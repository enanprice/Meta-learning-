Command Words and How Marks Are Awarded

What "show that", "hence", "deduce" and "state" actually require, and how to write answers that collect every available mark.

Study time: 1 hourNeeds first: None
On this page
  • Why this matters
  • 1. The command words
  • 2. "Show that" — the highest-value command word
  • 3. How marks are actually allocated
  • 4. Accuracy and rounding
  • 5. Writing answers examiners can mark
  • 6. Time management

Why this matters#

You can understand a topic perfectly and still lose a third of the marks on it, by answering a different question from the one asked or by not writing down the working the mark scheme is looking for.

Command words are not decoration. Each one specifies a required form of answer, and examiners mark against that specification.

1. The command words#

What each one demands

CommandWhat's requiredCommon mistake
Write down / StateThe answer alone, no working. Usually 1 mark.Wasting time showing working
Find / Calculate / DetermineThe answer with working.Answer only, losing method marks
Show thatFull working leading to the given result. The result is printed, so the marks are entirely for the steps.Jumping steps; or working backwards from the answer
ProveA complete logical argument, valid for all cases, with a concluding statement.Verifying with examples
HenceYou must use the previous part.Starting from scratch
Hence or otherwiseUsing the previous part is the intended (easier) route, but another method is allowed.Ignoring a much quicker route
DeduceDraw a conclusion from what you've just established, usually with little or no new calculation.Doing a whole fresh calculation
Explain / Give a reasonWords, not algebra. Usually in context.A calculation with no sentence

2. "Show that" — the highest-value command word#

Why "show that" questions are a gift and a trap

The gift: the answer is printed, so you can use it in the next part even if you can't derive it. Always do the later parts.

The trap: because the answer is given, every mark is for the working. An answer that mysteriously arrives at the printed result with two lines missing scores almost nothing.

Write more steps than feel necessary. Show the substitution, show the rearrangement, show the cancellation. And finish with the printed result, written out, followed by $\blacksquare$ or "as required".

The "show that" test

Read your working back and ask: could a competent student who hadn't seen the answer follow this to the result?

If any line makes a jump that requires knowing where you're heading, put the intermediate step in. That's exactly the line the mark scheme is looking for.

3. How marks are actually allocated#

The three mark types

  • M (Method): for a correct method, even with wrong numbers in it. You can lose the A mark and

still get the M.

  • A (Accuracy): for a correct answer or step. Dependent on the M mark — no method, no accuracy

mark.

  • B (Independent): a standalone correct statement, not dependent on anything else.

The single most profitable habit in the whole exam

Because A marks depend on M marks, writing the method down is worth more than getting the right answer.

A 6-mark question is typically 3–4 M marks and 2–3 A marks. A student who writes full method and makes one arithmetic slip scores 4 or 5. A student who does it all in their head and writes a wrong answer scores 0.

So: write the formula, write the substitution, then write the answer. Three lines, on every question.

Follow-through (ft)#

If you make an error early and then use your wrong value correctly in later parts, you usually still get the later marks. This is called follow-through, and it means:

  • Never abandon a question because you think part (a) is wrong. Carry on with your value.
  • Never leave a later part blank because you couldn't do an earlier one — use the printed "show

that" result, or make a sensible assumption and say so.

4. Accuracy and rounding#

The rules

  • Give 3 significant figures unless told otherwise.
  • Except in mechanics using $g = 9.8$, where 2 significant figures is expected.
  • Never round intermediate values. Carry full accuracy in your calculator (use ANS or store to

a variable) and round only the final answer.

  • "Exact value", "in surd form", "in terms of $\pi$", "leave your answer as a fraction" all mean

no decimals.

Premature rounding

If you round to 3 s.f. midway and then do three more operations, the final answer is often wrong in the third significant figure — and the accuracy mark is lost on a question you fully understood.

This is the single most common way that competent students drop marks. Use ANS.

5. Writing answers examiners can mark#

Habits that collect marks

  • Define your variables. "Let $X$ be the number of faulty items" is a mark in statistics.
  • State the distribution. "$X\sim B(20,0.3)$" is a mark.
  • Label your diagram. Forces, distances, the positive direction.
  • Say which rule you're using. "Using the product rule:" earns the method mark even if the

differentiation slips.

  • Answer in context. "The mean mass has increased" beats "reject $H_0$".
  • Include units. Every time, in mechanics and in any modelling question.
  • Reject invalid solutions with a reason. "$t = -0.6$ is rejected as time cannot be negative."
  • Underline or box your final answer, so the examiner can find it.

Crossing out

If you cross something out and don't replace it, it cannot be marked. If you're not sure which of two attempts is right, leave both — examiners mark the better one unless you've deleted it.

The exception: if you've written two different final answers and haven't indicated which you mean, you may be marked on the wrong one. Make your intention clear.

6. Time management#

1.2 minutes per mark. That's 200 marks in 240 minutes for the pure papers.

A workable exam strategy

  1. Two minutes at the start to skim the paper and note anything that looks unusual.
  2. Work through in order, but skip anything that doesn't come within 30 seconds of starting. Mark it with a star and move on.
  3. On your second pass, return to the starred ones with fresh eyes. It's remarkable how often a question that looked impossible becomes obvious.
  4. Leave 10 minutes at the end to fill in blanks, check units, and check that you've answered what was asked (interpolation vs extrapolation; distance vs displacement; coefficient vs term).
  5. Never leave a blank. A sensible attempt with the right method scores; nothing scores nothing.

The 3-minute rule

If you've spent 3 minutes over the time a question is worth and you're not close, the expected value of another minute there is lower than the expected value of a minute on an unattempted question later in the paper.

Students lose far more marks to unfinished papers than to wrong answers. Moving on is a skill, and it's worth practising deliberately in timed papers.
