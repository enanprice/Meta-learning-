Building a Revision Plan That Works

How to structure two years of maths revision, use past papers properly, and keep an error log that actually raises your grade.

Study time: 1 hourNeeds first: None

The honest starting point#

Most maths revision fails for one reason: it feels like work without being work.

Re-reading notes, copying out worked examples, highlighting a textbook, watching videos — all of these produce the warm sensation of understanding and almost none of the ability to produce an answer under pressure. You've been shown the method. You haven't retrieved it.

The techniques below are less comfortable and considerably more effective. That trade is the whole thing.

1. The four principles#

What the evidence actually supports

1. Retrieval practice. Closing the book and producing the method from memory. This is the single most effective study technique there is, and it's the one that feels worst, because you keep failing during it. The failing is the mechanism.

2. Spacing. Revisiting a topic after a gap, repeatedly, rather than hammering it once. Four 20-minute sessions across a month beat one 80-minute session, by a wide margin.

3. Interleaving. Mixing topics within a session. It feels chaotic and produces worse practice performance — and substantially better exam performance, because exams don't tell you which method to use. Mixed practice is how you learn to choose.

4. Error logging. Recording every dropped mark and why. Your error log is the most personalised, highest-value revision document you will ever own.

Why the effective methods feel worse

Re-reading is easy because the information is right there. Retrieval is hard because you have to generate it. But generating it is what builds the retrieval path — and the exam is a retrieval task, not a recognition task.

This is called desirable difficulty. If your revision feels smooth and pleasant, it's probably not doing much. If it feels effortful and you keep getting stuck, that's the sensation of learning.

The practical implication: judge a revision session by how much you produced, not by how much you covered.

2. A structure for the two years#

During Year 12 and 13 (ongoing)#

  • After each new topic: do the practice questions on that topic's page here, cold, on paper.
  • Three days later: redo the two you got wrong. This one habit is worth more than any amount of

end-of-year cramming.

  • Every few weeks: a mixed set of 6–8 questions drawn from different topics.

Six months out#

  • Work through every topic once, in order, doing the practice questions.
  • Build your formula sheet (see EX1) and start testing yourself on it weekly.
  • Start your error log.

Three months out#

  • Switch the balance to past papers, one full paper a week under timed conditions.
  • Use your error log to direct topic revision — go back to the pages here for whatever it keeps

flagging.

Final month#

  • Two full papers a week, timed, in exam conditions.
  • Re-do questions you got wrong from earlier papers. Not new papers — the ones you failed at.
  • Re-read your error log rather than your notes.

Final week#

  • No new material. Formula sheet, error log, and a couple of papers you've already done.
  • Sleep. Genuinely — sleep deprivation costs more marks than an extra evening of revision gains.

3. How to use a past paper properly#

The four-stage past paper method

Stage 1 — Do it properly. Full paper, timed, no notes, no calculator hunting, no phone. Two hours means two hours. Papers done in 20-minute chunks with the textbook open teach you nothing about exam performance.

Stage 2 — Mark it honestly. Use the official mark scheme. Award marks the way the scheme does, not the way you'd like. If you didn't write the method down, you don't get the M mark, however clearly you had it in your head.

Stage 3 — Classify every dropped mark. This is the stage everyone skips and it's the one that matters. For each lost mark, write down which of these it was:

  • Knowledge gap — I didn't know the method.
  • Application error — I knew the method but couldn't see it applied here.
  • Arithmetic/algebra slip — I knew what to do and made a mistake doing it.
  • Communication — I had the right answer but didn't write down what the scheme wanted.
  • Time — I ran out.

Stage 4 — Act on the classification. Each category has a different fix:

  • Knowledge gaps → go back to the topic page and re-learn it.
  • Application errors → do more mixed, unsignposted practice on that topic.
  • Slips → slow down, write more steps, use ANS.
  • Communication → re-read EX2 and practise writing full solutions.
  • Time → practise more timed papers, and get better at abandoning questions.

Doing a paper and just noting the score is close to worthless. The diagnosis is the value.

Don't burn all the papers early

There are a finite number of past papers, and a paper you've already seen is a much weaker test.

Keep at least three or four unseen papers for the final month, when you need an accurate read on where you actually are. Use older papers, specimen papers and textbook exercises for the bulk of your practice.

4. The error log#

What it is

One page (or one document) per module — Pure, Statistics, Mechanics — titled "mistakes I actually make".

Every time you lose a mark, add a line. Not the whole question: just the mistake and the fix.

Forgot $+c$ on an indefinite integral — again.

Used $P(X\leq 6)$ when the question said "at least 6"; should be $1 - P(X\leq 5)$.

Took $R = mg$ on a slope. It's $mg\cos\theta$.

Rounded the angle to 1 d.p. before using it; final answer wrong in the 3rd s.f.

Two-tailed test — forgot to halve the significance level.

Why the error log beats the textbook in the final week

Your errors are not random. Almost everyone has a personal set of five or six mistakes they make repeatedly, and those account for a large share of their dropped marks.

A textbook covers everything, most of which you can already do. Your error log covers only the things that are actually costing you marks. In the last week, reading it is worth more than reading anything else, because every line on it is a mark you have demonstrably lost before and can stop losing.

If a mistake appears three times on your log, it's not carelessness — it's a habit, and it needs a deliberate counter-habit. "Write the inequality out in full before computing" is a counter-habit. "Be more careful" is not.

5. Where the grades actually separate#

Be honest about what you're aiming at

Roughly speaking:

  • Getting a C to a B is mostly about reliability: doing standard questions accurately, not

dropping easy marks, finishing the paper.

  • Getting a B to an A is mostly about the multi-step questions where no method is named, and

about the harder parts of calculus and trigonometry.

  • Getting an A to an A\* is mostly about synoptic questions that combine two or three topics,

"show that" proofs, and the ability to keep accuracy across a long chain of work.

If you're dropping marks on routine questions, doing harder questions won't help — fix the reliability first. If you're scoring well on routine questions but stalling on the last part of each question, that's when to seek out the synoptic and stretch problems (flagged throughout this resource).

6. A realistic weekly template#

For the three months before the exam, assuming around 5 hours of maths revision a week:

SessionLengthContent
190 minOne full past paper section, timed
260 minMark it, classify errors, update the error log
360 minTopic revision on whatever the log flagged — read the page, do the questions
460 minMixed practice: 8 questions from 8 different topics
530 minFormula sheet test + re-do two questions you got wrong last week

That's 5 hours, and every one of them involves producing maths rather than reading it.

The most common revision mistake

Spending the whole time on the topics you enjoy, which are usually the ones you're already good at.

Your error log exists partly to override this instinct. Let it choose what you revise, not your mood.
