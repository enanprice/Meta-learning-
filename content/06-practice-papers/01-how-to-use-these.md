## What these are — and what they aren't

The three papers here are **original, exam-style** papers written to the Edexcel 9MA0 format:
Paper 1 and Paper 2 are Pure (100 marks each, 2 hours each), Paper 3 is Statistics and Mechanics
(50 + 50 marks, 2 hours).

They follow the real thing closely on the details that matter: mark allocations, the ramp from routine
openers to multi-step finishers, the command words, the "show that" structures that hand you a result
to use in the next part, and mark schemes written in Edexcel's own M/A/B notation.

:::warning These are not real past papers
They cannot be. Actual Edexcel past paper questions are Pearson's copyright, so this resource does not
reproduce them. Everything here is written from scratch in the same style.

That means you should treat these as **high-quality practice**, not as a predictor. Use them alongside
the real papers, not instead of them — and the next section tells you exactly where to get those.
:::

## Where to get the real past papers, free

You should be doing real papers. They're free and there are a lot of them.

:::key The sources worth knowing
- **Pearson's own Past Papers site** — the official source. Search for "Pearson qualifications past
  papers" and filter to A Level Mathematics (9MA0). You get the question paper, the mark scheme and
  the examiner's report for each series.
- **Your exam centre.** Schools have access to **secure materials** through Pearson's portal —
  additional practice papers and mock sets that are not on the public site. Ask your teacher; most
  will hand them over if you ask for them specifically.
- **The legacy specification (6663 etc.)** is a different syllabus but a great deal of the pure
  content is unchanged. C1–C4, S1 and M1 papers are a large extra bank of perfectly good practice.
- **Specimen and sample assessment materials** for 9MA0, published when the specification launched.
:::

:::insight Read the examiner's reports — almost nobody does
Every past paper comes with an examiner's report describing what candidates actually did wrong. They
are blunt, specific and enormously useful: *"many candidates failed to reject the negative root",*
*"the majority omitted the constant of integration",* *"a significant number used degrees throughout".*

That document is a free list of the traps, written by the people who set them. Reading the report for
a paper you've just sat is worth more than sitting another paper.
:::

## How to sit one of these properly

:::method The rules
1. **Two hours, one sitting.** Not two hours spread over an evening. The thing being tested is partly
   your stamina and your time allocation, and neither shows up in a paper done in chunks.
2. **Formula booklet only.** No notes, no textbook, no looking anything up. If you can't remember it,
   that's data — leave a gap and note it afterwards.
3. **The calculator you'll use in the exam.** Not a different one, and not your phone.
4. **Paper and pen.** Write everything you'd write in the real thing, including the method lines you'd
   be tempted to skip.
5. **Mark it yourself, honestly**, against the separate mark scheme — and follow the rules in
   *Exam Skills → Command Words and How Marks Are Awarded*. If you didn't write the method down, you
   don't get the M mark, however clearly you had it in your head.
6. **Classify every dropped mark** — knowledge gap, application error, slip, communication, or time.
   That classification is the entire point of the exercise.
:::

:::warning Don't mark generously
The single fastest way to waste a practice paper is to award yourself marks for things you "basically
had". The mark scheme is the standard; your opinion of your own working is not.

If you're unsure whether something earns the mark, assume it doesn't, and write the question down as
one to look at again.
:::

## Timing

**1.2 minutes per mark.** For a 100-mark paper in 120 minutes, that's the whole budget with nothing
spare.

| Marks | Target time |
|---|---|
| 4 | ~5 min |
| 6 | ~7 min |
| 8 | ~10 min |
| 10 | ~12 min |

If you're three minutes past the budget on a question and not close, **leave it, star it, and come
back**. Students lose far more marks to unfinished papers than to wrong answers.

## When to sit them

Not yet, if you're mid-course. A full paper is only a useful measurement once you've covered most of
the specification; before that it mostly measures what you haven't been taught.

A workable schedule for the final three months:

- **Three months out:** one paper a fortnight, plus topic revision driven by what they expose.
- **Final month:** one or two a week, plus **re-doing the questions you got wrong** from earlier ones.
- **Final week:** no new papers. Re-read your error log and redo two or three questions you've
  previously failed.

And keep at least three **unseen real papers** back for the last month. A paper you've already worked
through is a much weaker measurement, and in the final weeks you want an honest read on where you are.
