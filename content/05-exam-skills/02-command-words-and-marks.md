## Why this matters

You can understand a topic perfectly and still lose a third of the marks on it, by answering a
different question from the one asked or by not writing down the working the mark scheme is looking
for.

Command words are not decoration. Each one specifies a **required form of answer**, and examiners mark
against that specification.

---

## 1. The command words

:::key What each one demands
| Command | What's required | Common mistake |
|---|---|---|
| **Write down** / **State** | The answer alone, no working. Usually 1 mark. | Wasting time showing working |
| **Find** / **Calculate** / **Determine** | The answer **with working**. | Answer only, losing method marks |
| **Show that** | Full working leading to the **given** result. The result is printed, so the marks are entirely for the steps. | Jumping steps; or working backwards from the answer |
| **Prove** | A complete logical argument, valid for **all** cases, with a concluding statement. | Verifying with examples |
| **Hence** | You **must** use the previous part. | Starting from scratch |
| **Hence or otherwise** | Using the previous part is the intended (easier) route, but another method is allowed. | Ignoring a much quicker route |
| **Deduce** | Draw a conclusion from what you've just established, usually with little or no new calculation. | Doing a whole fresh calculation |
| **Explain** / **Give a reason** | Words, not algebra. Usually in **context**. | A calculation with no sentence |
| **Sketch** | Correct shape, key features labelled with coordinates, asymptotes labelled with equations. Not plotted accurately. | Unlabelled curve |
| **Plot** / **Draw** | Accurate, to scale, using the given axes. | Freehand sketch |
| **Verify** | Substitute the given value and show it works. | Solving from scratch |
| **Comment on** | An evaluative sentence in context, usually about a model's validity. | "It's fine" |
:::

## 2. "Show that" — the highest-value command word

:::warning Why "show that" questions are a gift and a trap
**The gift:** the answer is printed, so you can use it in the next part even if you can't derive it.
Always do the later parts.

**The trap:** because the answer is given, **every mark is for the working**. An answer that
mysteriously arrives at the printed result with two lines missing scores almost nothing.

Write more steps than feel necessary. Show the substitution, show the rearrangement, show the
cancellation. And finish with the printed result, written out, followed by $\blacksquare$ or "as
required".
:::

:::insight The "show that" test
Read your working back and ask: *could a competent student who hadn't seen the answer follow this to
the result?*

If any line makes a jump that requires knowing where you're heading, put the intermediate step in.
That's exactly the line the mark scheme is looking for.
:::

## 3. How marks are actually allocated

:::key The three mark types
- **M (Method):** for a correct method, **even with wrong numbers in it**. You can lose the A mark and
  still get the M.
- **A (Accuracy):** for a correct answer or step. **Dependent on the M mark** — no method, no accuracy
  mark.
- **B (Independent):** a standalone correct statement, not dependent on anything else.
:::

:::insight The single most profitable habit in the whole exam
Because A marks depend on M marks, **writing the method down is worth more than getting the right
answer**.

A 6-mark question is typically 3–4 M marks and 2–3 A marks. A student who writes full method and makes
one arithmetic slip scores 4 or 5. A student who does it all in their head and writes a wrong answer
scores 0.

So: write the formula, write the substitution, then write the answer. Three lines, on every question.
:::

### Follow-through (ft)

If you make an error early and then use your wrong value **correctly** in later parts, you usually
still get the later marks. This is called **follow-through**, and it means:

- **Never abandon a question** because you think part (a) is wrong. Carry on with your value.
- **Never leave a later part blank** because you couldn't do an earlier one — use the printed "show
  that" result, or make a sensible assumption and say so.

## 4. Accuracy and rounding

:::key The rules
- **Give 3 significant figures** unless told otherwise.
- **Except in mechanics using $g = 9.8$**, where **2 significant figures** is expected.
- **Never round intermediate values.** Carry full accuracy in your calculator (use `ANS` or store to
  a variable) and round only the final answer.
- **"Exact value", "in surd form", "in terms of $\pi$", "leave your answer as a fraction"** all mean
  **no decimals**.
:::

:::warning Premature rounding
If you round to 3 s.f. midway and then do three more operations, the final answer is often wrong in the
third significant figure — and the accuracy mark is lost on a question you fully understood.

This is the single most common way that competent students drop marks. Use `ANS`.
:::

## 5. Writing answers examiners can mark

:::method Habits that collect marks
- **Define your variables.** "Let $X$ be the number of faulty items" is a mark in statistics.
- **State the distribution.** "$X\sim B(20,0.3)$" is a mark.
- **Label your diagram.** Forces, distances, the positive direction.
- **Say which rule you're using.** "Using the product rule:" earns the method mark even if the
  differentiation slips.
- **Answer in context.** "The mean mass has increased" beats "reject $H_0$".
- **Include units.** Every time, in mechanics and in any modelling question.
- **Reject invalid solutions with a reason.** "$t = -0.6$ is rejected as time cannot be negative."
- **Underline or box your final answer**, so the examiner can find it.
:::

:::warning Crossing out
If you cross something out and don't replace it, it **cannot** be marked. If you're not sure which of
two attempts is right, leave **both** — examiners mark the better one unless you've deleted it.

The exception: if you've written two different final answers and haven't indicated which you mean,
you may be marked on the wrong one. Make your intention clear.
:::

## 6. Time management

**1.2 minutes per mark.** That's 200 marks in 240 minutes for the pure papers.

:::method A workable exam strategy
1. **Two minutes at the start** to skim the paper and note anything that looks unusual.
2. Work through in order, but **skip anything that doesn't come within 30 seconds of starting**. Mark
   it with a star and move on.
3. On your second pass, return to the starred ones with fresh eyes. It's remarkable how often a
   question that looked impossible becomes obvious.
4. **Leave 10 minutes at the end** to fill in blanks, check units, and check that you've answered what
   was asked (interpolation vs extrapolation; distance vs displacement; coefficient vs term).
5. **Never leave a blank.** A sensible attempt with the right method scores; nothing scores nothing.
:::

:::insight The 3-minute rule
If you've spent 3 minutes over the time a question is worth and you're not close, the expected value of
another minute there is lower than the expected value of a minute on an unattempted question later in
the paper.

Students lose far more marks to unfinished papers than to wrong answers. Moving on is a skill, and it's
worth practising deliberately in timed papers.
:::
