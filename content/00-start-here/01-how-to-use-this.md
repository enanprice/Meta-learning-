## What this is

This is a complete set of topic guides for **Pearson Edexcel A Level Mathematics (9MA0)** — the whole
two-year course, Year 12 and Year 13, Pure, Statistics and Mechanics.

Every topic guide follows the same shape:

1. **Why this topic exists** — what problem it solves, and where it plugs into the rest of the course.
2. **The ideas, built up in order** — each new bit of machinery explained from the ground up, with the
   reasoning shown, not just the rule stated.
3. **Worked examples** — full solutions with the thinking written out, including the choices you have
   to make and *why* you make them.
4. **Common mistakes** — the specific, predictable ways students drop marks on this topic.
5. **In the exam** — what questions actually look like, what examiners want to see written down.
6. **Practice questions** — graded from routine to exam-standard, each with a full worked solution
   behind a toggle.

## How to actually use it (read this bit)

Here is the uncomfortable truth about maths revision: **reading a worked solution feels like learning
and mostly isn't.** When you read someone else's solution, your brain goes "yes, obviously, I could
have done that." That feeling is called *fluency illusion* and it is the single biggest reason people
walk out of a maths exam surprised by their own grade.

So the rule for every practice question on this site:

:::key The one rule
Attempt the question **on paper, fully, to an answer**, before you open the solution. If you get
stuck, note *exactly where* you got stuck, then open the solution and read only until you're unstuck.
Close it. Finish the question yourself.
:::

A workable routine for one topic:

1. Read the explanation sections properly once. Slowly. Pen in hand.
2. Cover each worked example's solution and try it first. Then compare, line by line.
3. Do the practice questions cold, on paper, timed roughly at 1.2 minutes per mark.
4. Mark yourself honestly against the solution. Write down every mark you dropped and *why*.
5. Come back to that topic **3 days later** and redo the two questions you got wrong. This is the
   step everyone skips and it's the step that does the work.

## The four things that actually raise a maths grade

- **Spacing.** Revisiting a topic after a gap beats re-reading it now. A topic done once and revisited
  three times over a term will outperform a topic hammered for four hours in one sitting.
- **Retrieval.** Closing the book and producing the method from memory is the thing that builds recall.
  Looking at the method is not.
- **Interleaving.** Mixing topics in one session (a bit of trig, a bit of integration, a bit of
  mechanics) feels worse and works better, because exams don't tell you which method to use — mixed
  practice is where you learn to *choose* a method.
- **Error logs.** Keep one page per module titled "mistakes I actually make". Every dropped mark goes
  on it. Before an exam, that page is worth more than the whole textbook.

## Marking yourself honestly

Examiners give marks in three flavours, and you should mark yourself the same way:

| Mark | What it's for | What it means for you |
|---|---|---|
| **M** | Method | You used a correct method, even with a wrong number in it |
| **A** | Accuracy | The answer/step is correct — and only available if the M mark was earned |
| **B** | Independent | A standalone correct statement (e.g. writing down a correct value) |

The practical consequence: **write down the method even when you're not sure of the arithmetic.**
A correct method with a slip still scores most of the marks. A right answer with no working, on a
"show that" question, scores zero.

## Notation on this site

- $\Rightarrow$ means "implies"; $\iff$ means "if and only if" (implies both ways).
- $\therefore$ means "therefore". Use it sparingly in exams — words are clearer.
- Angles are in degrees when written $30^\circ$ and in radians otherwise. Year 13 pure is radians by
  default; get used to that early.
- "3 s.f." means three significant figures. Unless a question says otherwise, **give exact answers
  where possible, and round only at the very end** to 3 s.f.

:::warning The single most expensive habit
Rounding in the middle of a calculation. Carry full accuracy in your calculator's memory (use `ANS`
and stored variables) and round once, at the end. Premature rounding is the most common way to lose
an accuracy mark on a question you completely understood.
:::

## Kit you need

- A calculator that does numerical integration, numerical differentiation, matrices, statistical
  distributions and equation solving. The Casio fx-991CW / fx-991EX (ClassWiz) or fx-CG50 are the
  standard choices. **Learn its statistical distribution menus properly** — in Year 13 stats, the
  calculator can do inverse normal calculations that are painful by hand.
- The Edexcel formula booklet. Print it. Know what is in it (so you don't memorise those) and what
  is *not* in it (so you do memorise those). There's a whole guide to this in Exam Skills.
- Paper. Lots of it. Maths is not a screen activity.
