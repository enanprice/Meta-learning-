Person · 1457–1509

Henry VII

First Tudor king, 1485–1509.

Who they were

Henry Tudor spent 14 years in exile in Brittany and France and won the throne in battle with a weak claim through his mother, Margaret Beaufort. He spent his reign making it safe. He married Elizabeth of York, crushed the pretenders Lambert Simnel and Perkin Warbeck, and controlled the nobility through bonds and recognisances: legal promises that cost huge sums if broken.

He avoided expensive wars, made treaties that protected trade and won recognition abroad, and died leaving a secure throne and a healthy treasury.

Fact file

  • Battle of Stoke (1487) ended Simnel's rising: the last battle of the Wars of the Roses.
  • Perkin Warbeck, backed by Burgundy and Scotland, troubled him from 1491 to 1499.
  • The Council Learned in the Law enforced bonds; Empson and Dudley became hated as its agents.
  • Treaty of Medina del Campo (1489) agreed the marriage of Arthur to Catherine of Aragon.
  • Treaty of Etaples (1492): France paid him a pension and dropped Warbeck.
  • Cornish rebellion (1497) against war taxation was defeated at Blackheath.
  • Daughter Margaret married James IV of Scotland in 1503, which led eventually to the Stuart succession.

Timeline

  1. 1485Wins at Bosworth.
  2. 1486Marries Elizabeth of York.
  3. 1487Stoke.
  4. 1492Treaty of Etaples.
  5. 1497Cornish rebellion.
  6. 1499Warbeck executed.
  7. 1502Prince Arthur dies.
  8. 1509Dies.

Significance and connotations

What mentioning this in an essay signals, and the arguments it supports.

  • Security first. Nearly every Henry VII essay turns on how far each policy (marriage, finance, foreign policy, the nobility) served the security of the dynasty.
  • "New monarchy" or old? Historians argue whether his methods were new or a firmer use of Yorkist techniques.
  • Avarice. His financial methods made him rich and feared, but unpopular by 1509.

Person · 1491–1547

Henry VIII

King 1509–1547; broke with Rome and married six times.

Who they were

The athletic, cultured second son of Henry VII wanted glory, especially in war with France. He left much of the work of government to Wolsey in the 1510s and 1520s. Needing a male heir, he sought an annulment of his marriage to Catherine of Aragon. When the Pope would not grant it, he broke with Rome and made himself Supreme Head of the Church of England.

His later reign brought the dissolution of the monasteries, wars with France and Scotland that drained the treasury, and the debasement of the coinage.

Fact file

  • Wives: Catherine of Aragon, Anne Boleyn, Jane Seymour, Anne of Cleves, Catherine Howard, Catherine Parr.
  • Battle of the Spurs and victory over the Scots at Flodden, both 1513.
  • Fidei Defensor (Defender of the Faith), a papal title won in 1521 for attacking Luther.
  • Captured Boulogne in 1544 at enormous cost.
  • Debased the coinage from 1544, which fuelled inflation.

Timeline

  1. 1509King; marries Catherine.
  2. 1513Spurs and Flodden.
  3. 1527Seeks annulment.
  4. 1533Marries Anne Boleyn.
  5. 1534Act of Supremacy.
  6. 1537Son Edward born.
  7. 1544Captures Boulogne.
  8. 1547Dies.

Significance and connotations

What mentioning this in an essay signals, and the arguments it supports.

  • King or ministers? Was Henry in control, or did Wolsey and Cromwell drive policy? The central question for this aspect.
  • Faith or politics? Henry stayed Catholic in doctrine (the Six Articles, 1539) while rejecting the Pope. This is evidence that the Reformation was about power, not belief.

Person · c.1473–1530

Thomas Wolsey

Cardinal and Lord Chancellor; Henry's chief minister 1515–1529.

Who they were

A butcher's son from Ipswich who rose through the Church, Wolsey ran government and foreign policy for nearly 15 years. As Lord Chancellor, cardinal and papal legate he controlled both Church and State. He built Hampton Court, made enemies among the nobility, and failed in the one task that mattered most to Henry: the annulment. He fell in 1529 and died in 1530 on his way to face treason charges.

Fact file

  • Lord Chancellor and cardinal, 1515; papal legate a latere, 1518.
  • Treaty of London (1518): a European peace with England at its centre.
  • Field of the Cloth of Gold (1520).
  • The Amicable Grant (1525), a forced tax, met open resistance and was dropped.
  • The Blackfriars legatine court (1529) failed to grant the annulment.

Timeline

  1. 1515Lord Chancellor; cardinal.
  2. 1518Legate; Treaty of London.
  3. 1525Amicable Grant fails.
  4. 1529Falls from power.
  5. 1530Dies at Leicester.

Significance and connotations

What mentioning this in an essay signals, and the arguments it supports.

  • "Alter rex" (the other king). Shorthand for a minister who seemed to rule in place of the king.
  • The limits of royal finance. The Amicable Grant shows that even the Tudors could not tax without consent.

Person · c.1485–1540

Thomas Cromwell

Chief minister 1532–1540; engineered the break with Rome.

Who they were

A blacksmith's son who had been a soldier, merchant and lawyer, Cromwell served Wolsey and then rose to run Henry's government. He used Parliament to make the break with Rome legal, organised the survey of Church wealth and the dissolution of the monasteries, and reformed administration. He arranged Henry's disastrous marriage to Anne of Cleves and was executed in 1540.

Fact file

  • Drafted the Act in Restraint of Appeals (1533): "this realm of England is an empire".
  • Vicegerent in Spirituals (1535): the king's deputy over the Church.
  • Valor Ecclesiasticus (1535): survey of Church wealth.
  • Executed on 28 July 1540.

Timeline

  1. 1532Leading minister.
  2. 1533Act in Restraint of Appeals.
  3. 1535Vicegerent; Valor Ecclesiasticus.
  4. 1536–40Dissolution.
  5. 1540Anne of Cleves; execution.

Significance and connotations

What mentioning this in an essay signals, and the arguments it supports.

  • "Tudor revolution in government". G.R. Elton's thesis that Cromwell modernised government. Critics say he overstated it.
  • Statute law. Using Parliament made it a partner in royal power, a long-term change.

Person · 1537–1553

Edward VI

Boy king 1547–1553; reign of Protestant reform under Somerset and Northumberland.

Who they were

Edward became king at nine. His uncle, Edward Seymour (Duke of Somerset), ruled as Protector until rebellions in 1549 brought him down. John Dudley (Duke of Northumberland) then led the council. Both pushed Protestant reform: Prayer Books in English in 1549 and 1552. Dying at 15, Edward tried to pass the crown to his Protestant cousin Lady Jane Grey.

Fact file

  • Somerset: Protector 1547–49; costly war in Scotland.
  • Act of Uniformity and first Book of Common Prayer (1549).
  • Northumberland led the council 1550–53; second, more Protestant Prayer Book (1552).
  • The "Device for the Succession" named Lady Jane Grey.

Timeline

  1. 1547King; Somerset Protector.
  2. 1549Prayer Book; rebellions; Somerset falls.
  3. 1552Second Prayer Book.
  4. 1553Dies; Lady Jane Grey proclaimed.

Significance and connotations

What mentioning this in an essay signals, and the arguments it supports.

  • Minority rule. A test of whether the Tudor state could work without an adult monarch. That is central to the "mid-Tudor crisis" debate.

Person · 1516–1558

Mary I

Queen 1553–1558; restored Catholicism.

Who they were

Catherine of Aragon's daughter overturned the Grey plot with popular support and took the throne in 1553. She restored papal authority, married Philip of Spain, and had around 280 Protestants burned as heretics. Her war alongside Spain lost Calais, England's last French possession, in 1558. She died childless.

Fact file

  • Wyatt's rebellion (1554) against the Spanish marriage reached London.
  • Reconciled England with Rome in November 1554 through Cardinal Pole.
  • Burnings began in February 1555; Cranmer was burned in 1556.
  • Calais fell in January 1558.

Timeline

  1. 1553Takes the throne.
  2. 1554Wyatt's rebellion; marries Philip.
  3. 1555Burnings begin.
  4. 1558Calais lost; dies.

Significance and connotations

What mentioning this in an essay signals, and the arguments it supports.

  • "Bloody Mary". A label from Protestant propaganda (Foxe's Book of Martyrs). Revisionists such as Duffy argue her Catholic restoration was popular and effective.

Person · 1533–1603

Elizabeth I

Queen 1558–1603.

Who they were

Anne Boleyn's daughter inherited a divided country, a war with France and no money. She created a Protestant church with Catholic-looking features in 1559, refused to marry, and kept her council in balance. Catholic plots centred on Mary, Queen of Scots until Mary's execution in 1587. War with Spain followed, including the Armada of 1588. Her last decade saw bad harvests, war in Ireland, faction at court and Essex's failed rebellion.

Fact file

  • Rising of the Northern Earls (1569).
  • Excommunicated by the bull Regnans in Excelsis (1570).
  • Plots: Ridolfi (1571), Throckmorton (1583), Babington (1586).
  • Treaty of Nonsuch (1585): sent troops to help the Dutch rebels.
  • Nine Years' War in Ireland (1594–1603).
  • Golden Speech (1601) after a row in Parliament over monopolies.

Timeline

  1. 1558Queen.
  2. 1559Religious settlement.
  3. 1569Northern Rising.
  4. 1587Mary, Queen of Scots executed.
  5. 1588Armada.
  6. 1601Essex rebellion; Golden Speech.
  7. 1603Dies.

Significance and connotations

What mentioning this in an essay signals, and the arguments it supports.

  • Gloriana vs the troubled 1590s. Many essays turn on whether her last decade undermines her reputation.
  • Via media. Her religious middle way is the key to questions on the stability of the reign.

Person · 1520–1598

William Cecil, Lord Burghley

Elizabeth's chief minister for forty years.

Who they were

Cecil was Elizabeth's Secretary of State from 1558 and Lord Treasurer from 1572 until his death. Cautious and hard-working, he pushed for firm action against Mary, Queen of Scots and kept royal finances afloat. His son Robert succeeded him and managed James VI's accession in 1603.

Fact file

  • Secretary of State 1558–72; created Lord Burghley 1571.
  • Lord Treasurer 1572–98.
  • Rival of Robert Dudley, Earl of Leicester, at court.

Timeline

  1. 1558Secretary of State.
  2. 1571Lord Burghley.
  3. 1572Lord Treasurer.
  4. 1598Dies.

Significance and connotations

What mentioning this in an essay signals, and the arguments it supports.

  • Stability through continuity. His long service is a reason for the stability of Elizabeth's government; his death is a reason for the faction of the 1590s.

Event · 22 August 1485

The Battle of Bosworth

Henry Tudor wins the crown in battle.

What happened

Henry landed at Milford Haven with French-backed troops and met Richard III's larger army near Market Bosworth. The Stanleys held back until Sir William Stanley intervened for Henry. Richard was killed charging at Henry. Henry dated his reign from the day before the battle, so anyone who had fought for Richard could be called a traitor.

Fact file

  • Henry's army was about 5,000; Richard's was larger.
  • The Earl of Northumberland's troops did not fight for Richard.
  • Richard's remains were found under a Leicester car park in 2012.

Timeline

  1. 7 Aug 1485Henry lands in Wales.
  2. 22 Aug 1485Bosworth.
  3. Oct 1485Crowned.

Significance and connotations

What mentioning this in an essay signals, and the arguments it supports.

  • A weak title. Winning by battle meant he could lose the same way, which is the root of his concern with security.

Event · 1529–1534

The Break with Rome

Henry replaces the Pope as head of the English Church.

What happened

The Pope would not annul Henry's marriage, partly because Catherine's nephew, Emperor Charles V, controlled Rome. From 1529 the Reformation Parliament put pressure on the Church. The Act in Restraint of Appeals (1533) stopped appeals to Rome, which let Archbishop Cranmer annul the marriage in England. The Act of Supremacy (1534) made Henry Supreme Head of the Church. Thomas More and Bishop Fisher were executed in 1535 for refusing to accept it.

Fact file

  • Submission of the Clergy (1532).
  • Act in Restraint of Appeals (1533).
  • Act of Supremacy and Treasons Act (1534).
  • More and Fisher executed (1535).

Timeline

  1. 1527Annulment sought.
  2. 1529Reformation Parliament.
  3. 1533Appeals Act; Anne Boleyn crowned.
  4. 1534Act of Supremacy.
  5. 1535More and Fisher executed.

Significance and connotations

What mentioning this in an essay signals, and the arguments it supports.

  • Marriage, money or faith? The annulment triggered it; Church wealth and royal power were motives too, and evangelicals like Anne Boleyn and Cranmer pushed reform.

Event · 1536–1540

The Dissolution of the Monasteries

Henry seizes the Church's monastic wealth.

What happened

After the Valor Ecclesiasticus and visitations reporting corruption, the 1536 Act closed smaller monasteries worth under £200 a year. The larger houses followed, many "surrendering" under pressure, and a 1539 Act confirmed it. By 1540 around 800 religious houses had gone. Most land was sold to the gentry and nobility, which gave them a stake in the Reformation.

Fact file

  • Court of Augmentations (1536) handled the proceeds.
  • Monastic income was worth roughly £136,000 a year to the Crown.
  • Much of the wealth was spent on war in the 1540s.

Timeline

  1. 1535Valor Ecclesiasticus; visitations.
  2. 1536Smaller houses dissolved.
  3. 1539Act for larger houses.
  4. 1540Last houses close.

Significance and connotations

What mentioning this in an essay signals, and the arguments it supports.

  • Greed or reform? Mostly financial, but it also destroyed a core Catholic institution and made the change hard to reverse.

Event · 1536–1537

The Pilgrimage of Grace

The largest rising against Henry VIII.

What happened

Beginning in Lincolnshire in October 1536 and spreading through Yorkshire and the north, the rising was led by the lawyer Robert Aske. Around 30,000 rebels gathered at Doncaster, demanding the end of the dissolution, the removal of Cromwell, and a return to papal authority. The Duke of Norfolk promised a pardon and a parliament. After a fresh rising (Bigod's) in January 1537, Henry executed around 180 rebels, including Aske.

Fact file

  • The rebels' badge was the Five Wounds of Christ.
  • Demands included economic grievances: taxes and enclosure.

Timeline

  1. Oct 1536Lincolnshire and Yorkshire risings.
  2. Dec 1536Doncaster truce.
  3. Jan 1537Bigod's rising.
  4. 1537Executions.

Significance and connotations

What mentioning this in an essay signals, and the arguments it supports.

  • Religion vs economics. The classic debate over the causes of Tudor rebellions.
  • Loyal rebels. They blamed "evil counsellors", not the king.

Event · 1549

The Rebellions of 1549

The Western Rebellion and Kett's Rebellion in one summer.

What happened

In the south-west, Cornish and Devon rebels rose against the new English Prayer Book and besieged Exeter. In Norfolk, Robert Kett led about 16,000 rebels to camp on Mousehold Heath outside Norwich, protesting against enclosure and abuses by landlords. Both were crushed in August, Kett's at Dussindale by the Earl of Warwick (later Northumberland). The crisis brought down Somerset.

Fact file

  • Western rebels demanded the return of the Latin Mass.
  • Kett's rebels took Norwich, England's second city.
  • Several thousand were killed in the two risings.

Timeline

  1. Jun 1549Western rebellion begins.
  2. Jul 1549Kett's camp at Mousehold.
  3. Aug 1549Both crushed.
  4. Oct 1549Somerset falls.

Significance and connotations

What mentioning this in an essay signals, and the arguments it supports.

  • Crisis evidence. Two major risings at once is the strongest case for a "mid-Tudor crisis".
  • Two causes, two rebellions. Religion in the west and economics in the east.

Event · 1559

The Elizabethan Religious Settlement

A Protestant church with Catholic-looking features.

What happened

The Act of Supremacy made Elizabeth Supreme Governor (not "Head") of the Church. The Act of Uniformity restored a Prayer Book based on 1552 but kept vestments and some ceremony. Recusants were fined 12 pence for each Sunday they missed church. The Uniformity bill passed the Lords by only three votes; the Marian bishops opposed it and were replaced. The Thirty-Nine Articles (1563) set out its doctrine.

Fact file

  • Royal Injunctions (1559) set the rules for worship.
  • All but one Marian bishop refused the oath.
  • Puritans later attacked it for not going far enough.

Timeline

  1. 1559Acts of Supremacy and Uniformity.
  2. 1563Thirty-Nine Articles.
  3. 1570Excommunication.

Significance and connotations

What mentioning this in an essay signals, and the arguments it supports.

  • Via media. It was a compromise designed for stability, but it satisfied neither Catholics nor Puritans fully.

Person · 1566–1625

James I

King of Scotland from 1567 and of England 1603–1625.

Who they were

An experienced ruler and scholar, James believed in the divine right of kings and wrote about it. In England he faced a Crown that was short of money, and he made it worse with generous gifts to favourites, especially George Villiers, Duke of Buckingham. His relations with Parliament were often tense, over money, his prerogative and foreign policy, but they never broke down completely.

Fact file

  • Hampton Court Conference (1604) with Puritans; led to the King James Bible (1611).
  • The Great Contract (1610) would have swapped feudal dues for a fixed income; it failed.
  • The Addled Parliament (1614) passed no laws.
  • Parliament's Protestation (1621) asserted its right to free speech; James tore it out of the Commons Journal.
  • The Spanish Match, a planned marriage for Charles, collapsed in 1623.

  1. 1603English king.
  2. 1605Gunpowder Plot.
  3. 1610Great Contract fails.
  4. 1614Addled Parliament.
  5. 1621Protestation.
  6. 1625Dies.

Person · 1600–1649

Charles I

King 1625–1649; executed by his own subjects.

Who they were

Shy, formal and convinced of his divine right, Charles quarrelled with Parliament over war finance, Buckingham and religion, then ruled without it from 1629. His attempt to impose an English-style Prayer Book on Scotland in 1637 sparked a rebellion that forced him to recall Parliament in 1640. Mistrust between Charles and Parliament's leaders led to war in 1642.

Defeated in 1646, he kept negotiating with every side while plotting with the Scots. That brought the Second Civil War in 1648 and convinced the army he could never be trusted. He was tried and beheaded in January 1649.

  • Married the Catholic French princess Henrietta Maria in 1625.

  • Personal Rule 1629–40.
  • Attempted to arrest the Five Members, January 1642.
  • The Engagement with the Scots (December 1647) led to the Second Civil War.
  • Last word on the scaffold: "Remember".

Person · 1599–1658

Oliver Cromwell

Parliament's greatest general; Lord Protector 1653–1658.

Who they were

A Cambridgeshire gentleman and Puritan MP, Cromwell built a reputation as a cavalry commander, at Marston Moor (1644) and Naseby (1645). Person · 1630–1685

King 1660–1685; the "Merry Monarch" of the Restoration.

Who they were

What happened

Event · 1629–1640

The Personal Rule

Eleven years without Parliament.

What happened

Charles made peace with France (1629) and Spain (1630) and raised money without Parliament. He revived old feudal rights: distraint of knighthood, forest fines and monopolies. Above all he used Ship Money, extended from coastal counties to the whole country in 1635. It brought in about £200,000 a year at first. John Hampden's refusal led to a court case in 1637–38 that the Crown won by just 7 votes to 5, and payment fell sharply. Laud's church reforms ran alongside.

Fact file

  • Ship Money collection was close to 90% early on and had collapsed by 1639.
  • "Thorough": a policy of efficient central rule associated with Wentworth and Laud.

Event · 1640–1642

The Long Parliament and the Slide to War

Reform, mistrust, and the split into two sides.

What happened

In 1641 the Long Parliament, led by John Pym, dismantled the Personal Rule. It executed Strafford, abolished Star Chamber and the prerogative courts, declared Ship Money illegal, and passed a Triennial Act. Nearly everyone agreed on that. The split came over religion and control of the army. After the Irish rebellion (October 1641), the Grand Remonstrance listing Charles's misdeeds passed by only 159 to 148, and a royalist party formed. Charles's attempt to arrest the Five Members in January 1642 destroyed trust. Parliament's Militia Ordinance and the king's rejection of the Nineteen Propositions led to war.

Fact file

  • Triennial Act (February 1641).
  • Act against dissolving Parliament without its own consent (May 1641).
  • Star Chamber and High Commission abolished (July 1641).
  • Grand Remonstrance: 22 November 1641, 159–148.
  • Five Members: 4 January 1642.

Event · 1642–1648

The Civil Wars

Parliament defeats the king, twice.

What happened

The first battle, Edgehill (October 1642), was indecisive. Parliament gained the Scots as allies in 1643 (the Solemn League and Covenant) and won at Marston Moor in 1644. The Self-Denying Ordinance and the New Model Army under Fairfax, with Cromwell commanding the cavalry, delivered the decisive win at Naseby in June 1645. Charles surrendered to the Scots in 1646. A Second Civil War in 1648, with Scottish support for the king, was crushed at Preston.

Person · 1868–1918

Nicholas II

Tsar of Russia, 1894–1917, and the last Romanov ruler.

Who they were

Nicholas inherited the throne at 26 and a firm belief, drilled into him by his father and his tutor Pobedonostsev, that God had given him unlimited power to hold in trust for his heir. He was dutiful and devoted to his family, but indecisive, poorly informed about his own country, and suspicious of ministers who were cleverer than him.

He survived 1905 by making concessions he then clawed back. In 1915 he took personal command of the army, which tied him directly to every defeat and left the government in Petrograd to his wife. When the Petrograd garrison mutinied in February 1917, his generals told him to abdicate, and he did.

Fact file

  • Called the hopes of reformers "senseless dreams" in January 1895, months into his reign.
  • His coronation celebrations in 1896 included the Khodynka Field crush, in which over 1,300 died. He attended a ball that evening.
  • Granted the October Manifesto (1905) under pressure, then issued the Fundamental Laws (1906) restating his "supreme autocratic power".
  • Dissolved the first two Dumas within months.
  • Took command of the army at Mogilev in September 1915, 800 km from Petrograd.
  • Abdicated for himself and his son on 2 March 1917. His brother Michael refused the throne the next day.
  • Shot with his family by the Bolsheviks at Ekaterinburg in July 1918.

Timeline

  1. 1894Succeeds Alexander III; marries Alexandra of Hesse.
  2. 1896Coronation; Khodynka Field disaster.
  3. 1904War with Japan. Son Alexei born with haemophilia.
  4. 1905Bloody Sunday; October Manifesto.
  5. 1906Fundamental Laws; dissolves the First Duma.
  6. 1913Romanov tercentenary celebrations.
  7. 1915Takes personal command of the army.
  8. 1917Abdicates on 2 March.
  9. 1918Executed at Ekaterinburg, 16–17 July.

Significance and connotations

What mentioning this in an essay signals, and the arguments it supports.

  • Personal failure vs structural weakness. Using Nicholas lets you argue the regime fell because of a weak ruler. The counter is that any tsar would have struggled with war, a peasant land crisis and a growing working class. Good essays weigh both.
  • The "little father" myth. Peasants and workers traditionally blamed bad officials, not the Tsar. Bloody Sunday and the war destroyed that belief, so Nicholas personally became the target.
  • Wasted chances. 1905–06 and 1915 (the Progressive Bloc) were moments when sharing power with moderates might have saved the monarchy. He refused both times.
  • Commander-in-chief. Shorthand for the turning point when military defeat became the Tsar's personal responsibility.

A peasant mystic from Siberia, Rasputin gained the trust of Alexandra because he seemed to ease Alexei's haemophilia. Person · 1849–1915

Sergei Witte

Finance Minister 1892–1903; the architect of Russia's industrial boom.

Who they were

A former railway manager, Witte believed Russia must industrialise or become a "colony" of the West. As Finance Minister he used high tariffs, foreign loans, the gold standard and huge state railway projects to drive industry, paying for it with taxes on the peasantry.

Nicholas disliked him and moved him out in 1903. Recalled in 1905, he negotiated peace with Japan, drafted the October Manifesto and secured a large French loan before being dismissed again in 1906.

Fact file

  • Industrial growth averaged about 8% a year in the 1890s.
  • Put the rouble on the gold standard in 1897 to attract foreign investment.
  • Drove the Trans-Siberian Railway (begun 1891).
  • Introduced the state vodka monopoly from 1894, a major source of revenue.
  • Prime Minister October 1905 – April 1906.

Person · 1862–1911

Pyotr Stolypin

Prime Minister 1906–11: repression and land reform.

Who they were

A tough provincial governor, Stolypin became Interior Minister and then Prime Minister in 1906. His policy was "pacification first, then reform". Field courts-martial executed thousands of rebels; the noose became known as "Stolypin's necktie".

His land reforms let peasants leave the commune and consolidate their strips into private farms. He hoped to create a class of prosperous, loyal peasant farmers, a "wager on the strong". He said he needed twenty years of peace. He was shot in a Kiev theatre in September 1911.

Fact file

  • Field courts-martial (1906–07) executed over 1,000 people, with thousands more in later years.
  • Decree of 9 November 1906 allowed peasants to leave the commune.
  • By 1914 about 2–2.5 million households had applied to leave, but only around 10% of peasant households farmed fully consolidated holdings.
  • About 3 million peasants resettled in Siberia between 1906 and 1914.
  • Dissolved the Second Duma in June 1907 and changed the electoral law to favour landowners: the "Stolypin coup".
  • Assassinated by Dmitri Bogrov, a revolutionary who was also a police informer.

Timeline

  1. 1906Becomes Interior Minister, then Prime Minister. Land decree.
  2. 1907Dissolves Second Duma; new electoral law.
  3. 1910Land reform law confirmed by the Duma.
  4. 1911Assassinated in Kiev.

Significance and connotations

What mentioning this in an essay signals, and the arguments it supports.

  • Repression and reform together. He shows the regime could still act decisively after 1905, which supports arguments that tsarism was recovering before 1914.
  • "Twenty years of peace". The phrase lets you argue the war, not tsarism's flaws, cut reform short. The counter: progress was slow even before 1914.
  • Optimists vs pessimists. Historians who think tsarism could have survived lean on Stolypin; pessimists like Figes say the reforms were too little, too late.

Bolshevik leader; head of the Soviet government 1917–24.

Event · 1905–1907

The 1905 Revolution and the October Manifesto

Strikes, mutinies and peasant risings force the Tsar to promise a parliament.

What happened

Through 1905 Russia saw mass strikes, peasant attacks on landlords, national revolts in Poland and the Baltic, and mutinies like the battleship Potemkin in June. A general strike in October paralysed the country and workers set up the St Petersburg Soviet. On Witte's advice, Nicholas issued the October Manifesto on 17 October, promising an elected Duma and civil rights.

The Manifesto split the opposition. Liberals (Octobrists, and less fully the Kadets) accepted it. The army, back from the east and still loyal, crushed the Moscow rising in December and the Soviet leaders were arrested. Peasant unrest was put down by 1907.

Fact file

  • Potemkin mutiny: June 1905, in Odessa.
  • October general strike: railways, factories and offices stopped.
  • St Petersburg Soviet: October–December 1905, chaired by Trotsky.
  • October Manifesto: elected Duma, freedom of speech, assembly and conscience.
  • Moscow rising crushed in December 1905, with over 1,000 dead.
  • Redemption payments halved in 1906 and abolished from 1907.

  1. Jan 1905Bloody Sunday.
  2. Jun 1905Potemkin mutiny.
  3. Oct 1905General strike; Soviet formed; October Manifesto.
  4. Dec 1905Moscow rising crushed.

Event · 1906–1917

The Fundamental Laws and the Dumas

Russia's first parliament, granted and then hollowed out.

What happened

Days before the First Duma met, the Fundamental Laws (April 1906) declared that "supreme autocratic power" still belonged to the Tsar. He appointed ministers, controlled foreign policy and the army, and could rule by decree when the Duma was not sitting (Article 87).

The first two Dumas were radical and were dissolved within months. Stolypin then changed the electoral law, and the Third Duma (1907–12) was dominated by conservatives and ran its full term. The Fourth (1912–17) grew more critical, formed the Progressive Bloc in 1915, and its leaders formed the Provisional Government in 1917.

Fact file

  • First Duma: April–July 1906. Kadet majority; dissolved. The Vyborg Manifesto protest followed.
  • Second Duma: February–June 1907; dissolved.
  • June 1907 electoral law gave landowners far more weight than peasants and workers.
  • Third Duma 1907–12: the most cooperative.
  • Fourth Duma 1912–17: Progressive Bloc formed in August 1915.

  1. Apr 1906Fundamental Laws; First Duma.
  2. 1907Second Duma dissolved; new electoral law.
  3. 1907–12Third Duma.

Event · 1914–1917

The First World War

Total war breaks the army, the economy and the monarchy.

What happened

Russia entered the war in July 1914 amid patriotic enthusiasm. Defeat at Tannenberg in August 1914 and the Great Retreat of 1915 cost millions of men and huge territory. Brusilov's offensive in 1916 succeeded at first, then stalled at enormous cost.

At home, conscription took peasants from the land, the railways could not carry food to the cities, and the government printed money. Prices rose roughly fourfold. Petrograd went short of bread and fuel in the cold winter of 1916–17.

Fact file

  • Tannenberg (August 1914): around 30,000 killed or wounded and about 90,000 captured.

  • Great Retreat (1915): Poland and much of the western borderlands lost.
  • Brusilov Offensive (June 1916): early success, about a million Russian casualties.
  • Prices roughly quadrupled between 1914 and early 1917.

Event · 1918–1921

The Civil War

Reds against Whites, Greens and foreign intervention.

What happened

The war spread after the Czech Legion revolted in May 1918. The Whites were a mix of monarchists, liberals and some socialists, led by generals such as Kolchak in Siberia, Denikin in the south and Yudenich near Petrograd. Britain, France, the USA and Japan sent troops, though in small numbers. Peasant "Green" armies fought both sides.

The Reds held the central heartland, with Moscow, Petrograd, most industry and the railway hub. They had one leadership and one aim. The Whites were spread around the edges, divided, and associated with the return of the landlords. By the end of 1920 the main White armies were beaten. War with Poland ended with the Treaty of Riga in 1921.

  • Denikin reached Orel, under 400 km from Moscow, in October 1919.
  • Yudenich reached the outskirts of Petrograd in October 1919.
  • Deaths from fighting, terror, disease and famine are estimated at 7–10 million.

Event · 1921–1928

The New Economic Policy

A partial return to markets to rescue the economy.

What happened

Announced at the Tenth Party Congress in March 1921, NEP replaced requisitioning with a tax in kind. Peasants could sell their surplus, small businesses could trade for profit, and the state kept the "commanding heights": heavy industry, banks, railways and foreign trade. Lenin called it a step back to take two steps forward.

At the same Congress, factions within the party were banned. Economic freedom came with political tightening. NEP traders, the "Nepmen", and richer peasants (kulaks) did well, which angered many party members.

Fact file

  • Tax in kind (1921), later a money tax.
  • Small firms of under about 20 workers could be private.
  • Ban on factions: Tenth Party Congress, March 1921.
  • "Scissors crisis" (1923): industrial prices rose far above farm prices.
  • By the mid-1920s production was back near 1913 levels.

1894–1914

The Russian Economy, 1894–1914

Three full essay plans, a timeline, a statistics bank and historians.

OverviewNotesEssays

Three plans on three different questions. Each takes its own line of argument, so don't merge them into one essay. Every plan uses the same shape:

  • Introduction: define the key terms, set the context and state your judgement up front.
  • Three paragraphs: each one opens with a point that answers the question, gives specific evidence, explains how the evidence proves the point, deals with the counter-argument, and ends with a mini-judgement tied back to the question.
  • Conclusion: weigh the factors against each other and give your final judgement. Don't summarise.

About the numbers. Russian statistics from this period are disputed, and textbooks give slightly different figures (coal output in 1913 appears as anything from 35 to 36 million tonnes, for example). The figures below are the ones most often used in A-level textbooks. Before an exam, check them against your own textbook and use its versions. Examiners reward accurate, confident use of evidence, and they mark down figures that look invented.

Historians to name-drop (one line each is enough)#

  • Alexander Gerschenkron: the "relative backwardness" thesis. In a backward economy without a strong middle class or private capital, the state has to act as the engine of industrialisation. This supports the state-led argument.
  • Paul Gregory: an optimist. He argues national income and agricultural output grew faster than population (agriculture about 2% a year) and that the economy was healthier in 1914 than pessimists claim.
  • Orlando Figes: a pessimist. Stolypin's reforms were too little, too late, and the peasantry stayed alienated.

Essay 1: The extent of change#

Question: "The Russian economy was transformed in the years 1894–1914." Assess the validity of this view.#

Line of argument: Russia's industrial economy was transformed in scale. The structure of the economy as a whole was not: it stayed mostly agrarian, mostly peasant, and dependent on the state and foreign money. So "transformed" is valid for industry and invalid for the economy as a whole.

Introduction#

  • Define "transformed": a fundamental change in the structure of the economy, meaning what most people did for a living, where wealth came from and how production was organised. Rapid growth alone doesn't count.
  • Context: in 1894 Russia was a great power with a backward economy. The 1891–92 famine had just shown how vulnerable it was. Witte (Finance Minister 1892–1903) and later Stolypin (1906–11) tried to modernise it from above.
  • Judgement: heavy industry and transport changed dramatically. The economy as a whole was not transformed, because agriculture, the sector that employed more than 80% of the population, barely changed, and industrial growth depended on the state, foreign capital and peace. The best word for 1894–1914 is uneven rather than transformed.

Paragraph 1: Industry and infrastructure were transformed (for the view)#

  • Point: in heavy industry and transport the change was large enough to call a transformation.
  • Evidence:
    • Coal rose from 5.9m tonnes (1890) to 35.4m (1913), roughly six times over. Pig iron rose about fivefold, from 0.9m to 4.6m tonnes.
    • Industrial growth averaged about 8% a year in the 1890s and about 6% in 1908–13.
    • Track grew from about 30,600 km to about 70,200 km. The Trans-Siberian, begun in 1891, linked European Russia to the Pacific.
    • New industrial regions appeared: the Donbass for coal and iron, Baku for oil (the world's largest producer around 1900), and St Petersburg and Moscow for engineering and textiles.
    • Russia was roughly the fifth-largest industrial power by 1913.
  • Analysis: this was qualitative change as well as more output. Russia built a modern capital goods sector (coal, iron, steel, rail) almost from nothing in a single generation. Railways tied the national market together and opened up Siberian grain and Donbass coal. That is structural change in industry.
  • Counter: growth started from a very low base, so high percentages flatter the story. Per head, Russia still produced far less coal and iron than Britain, Germany or the USA.
  • Mini-judgement: within industry the view holds. Industry, though, was the smaller part of the economy.

Paragraph 2: Agriculture, the core of the economy, was not transformed (against the view)#

  • Point: the sector that employed most Russians changed only at the margins, which undermines the claim that "the Russian economy" was transformed.
  • Evidence:
    • More than 80% of the population were peasants (1897 census). The factory workforce was only about 3m out of about 170m by 1914.
    • Strip farming, the three-field system and the mir (commune) dominated. Yields stayed well below Western European levels.
    • Stolypin's 1906 decree: about 2–2.5m households applied to leave the commune, but only around 10% farmed consolidated holdings by 1914. Stolypin himself said he needed "twenty years of peace". He got about five before he was assassinated in 1911.
    • Population grew from about 125m to about 170m, so land hunger got worse.
  • Analysis: this is the strongest case against "transformed". Most Russians lived much as their parents had in 1894. Industry had been bolted onto a peasant economy without changing it. Witte's model actually depended on agriculture not changing: grain exports (about half of export earnings) and indirect taxes on peasant goods such as vodka, sugar and kerosene paid for industrialisation. Vyshnegradsky's line "we may go hungry, but we will export" sums it up.
  • Counter (Gregory): agricultural output grew about 2% a year, faster than population. The record harvest of 1913 and the end of redemption payments (1907) left better-off peasants (the kulaks) more prosperous.
  • Mini-judgement: there was real but limited improvement, and nothing like a transformation of the economy's foundations.

Paragraph 3: The way the economy worked stayed dependent (mostly against, with a caveat)#

  • Point: even where output changed, the motor of the economy didn't. Growth relied on the state and foreign capital rather than a self-sustaining domestic market. That is a sign of incomplete transformation.
  • Evidence:
    • Foreign investment rose from about 200m roubles (1890) to about 900m (1900) and about 2,000m (1914). The gold standard (1897) was adopted largely to attract it, mostly French loans.
    • The state was the main customer for rail and iron. When state orders and foreign credit dried up in the 1899–1903 recession, many firms collapsed.
    • Cartels and syndicates (Prodamet 1902, Produgol 1906) controlled prices rather than competing.
    • The caveat: after 1908 the boom was driven more by private Russian banks and domestic demand, for example consumer goods and agricultural machinery for richer peasants.
  • Analysis: an economy whose growth switches off when the state stops spending or Paris stops lending hasn't been transformed into a modern market economy. It has been artificially accelerated. The 1908–13 boom is the best evidence that it was starting to change, but six years isn't long enough to call it complete.
  • Mini-judgement: the 1908–13 shift shows transformation beginning, not finished.

Conclusion#

  • Weigh it up: the transformation of industry (P1) is real, but it was concentrated in a small modern sector employing a few million people. Agriculture (P2) carries more weight in the judgement because it was most of the economy and most of the people.
  • Final judgement: the view is valid for industry and infrastructure and invalid for the economy as a whole. Russia in 1914 had a modern industrial sector inside a traditional peasant economy. That is a dual economy, and dual economies are by definition not transformed. The social strain this produced (the 1912–14 strike wave and continuing land hunger) shows the change was destabilising rather than complete.

Essay 2: The fundamental cause of economic development#

Question: How far was state policy the main reason for Russian economic growth in the years 1894–1914?#

Line of argument: state policy was the main driver in 1894–1903, as Gerschenkron's model predicts. After 1905 its role shrank: foreign capital, private enterprise and better harvests did more of the work. The state stayed the most important single factor across the whole period because it created the conditions the other factors relied on, but it became less dominant over time.

Introduction#

  • Define: "state policy" means government action: tariffs, railway building, state orders, currency and tax policy, and the Stolypin land reforms. "Economic growth" means rising output in industry and agriculture.
  • Context: Russia had little private capital, a small middle class and a mostly illiterate peasantry (about 21% literacy in 1897). Gerschenkron argues that in that situation the state must replace the missing entrepreneurs.
  • Judgement: state policy was the main reason overall, but its importance changed over the period. It dominated under Witte. After 1905 its direct role faded, and growth came more from foreign capital, private banks and agriculture, all of which still relied on conditions the state had created.

Paragraph 1: State policy under Witte, 1894–1903 (main factor)#

  • Point: the 1890s boom was deliberately engineered by the state.
  • Evidence:
    • The 1891 Mendeleev tariff kept out foreign manufactures and made foreign firms build factories inside Russia.
    • State railway building: the Trans-Siberian, and track up from about 30,600 km to about 53,200 km by 1900. The state ordered the rails, locomotives and coal.
    • The 1897 gold standard stabilised the rouble.
    • Indirect taxes and the vodka monopoly (from 1894) funded investment. The vodka monopoly became one of the state's largest single sources of revenue.
    • Result: about 8% annual industrial growth. Coal went from 5.9m tonnes (1890) to 16.1m (1900).
  • Analysis: the state did more than help growth. It created demand (rail orders), protected supply (tariffs), and financed it (taxes and loans). Without that, private actors would not have invested in a country this risky. Witte's 1899 memorandum to Nicholas II explicitly argued industrialisation was needed so Russia would not become a "colony" of the West.
  • Counter: state-led growth was fragile. The 1899–1903 recession showed it could not sustain itself, and it squeezed the peasantry (the 1902 Poltava and Kharkov risings).
  • Mini-judgement: for the first decade, state policy was clearly the main reason.

Paragraph 2: Foreign capital (strong rival factor, but dependent on the state)#

  • Point: the money that actually built Russian industry was largely foreign, which challenges the idea that the state was the "main" reason.
  • Evidence:
    • Foreign investment went from about 200m to about 900m roubles in 1890–1900, and about 2,000m by 1914.
    • Foreign capital is often estimated at around a third or more of all industrial investment, and far higher in mining and metallurgy. French and Belgian capital dominated the Donbass, and the Nobels and Rothschilds were in Baku oil.
    • French state loans after the Franco-Russian alliance (1894) also funded the railways.
  • Analysis: foreign capital was a necessary condition, since Russia did not have the savings to fund growth on this scale. But it followed state policy. Investors came because of the gold standard, the tariff walls and the guaranteed state contracts. So this factor partly supports the state's case: the state was the reason the foreign money arrived.
  • Mini-judgement: this is the most important source of growth, but it was dependent on state policy rather than independent of it.

Paragraph 3: After 1905, private enterprise, agriculture and the market (state role declining)#

  • Point: the 1908–13 boom had a different engine. Private and market forces mattered more and direct state spending mattered less.
  • Evidence:
    • Industrial growth was about 6% a year in 1908–13, led by Russian joint-stock banks, consumer goods and agricultural machinery rather than state rail orders.
    • Agriculture: good harvests (a record in 1913), the end of redemption payments (1907), and the Peasant Land Bank helping richer peasants buy land. These raised rural demand for manufactured goods.
    • Kokovtsov balanced budgets and built up gold reserves. That was a more cautious state that stabilised the economy rather than driving it.
    • Stolypin's reforms were state policy, but their effect was limited (around 10% consolidated holdings by 1914). Good harvests probably did more than the reforms did.
  • Analysis: this is the strongest case against "main reason". By 1914 the economy was starting to grow from domestic demand, which is what Gregory emphasises. Even so, the state still set the framework: tariffs, the currency, railways already built, and the Land Bank.
  • Mini-judgement: after 1905 the state was an enabler more than an engine.

Conclusion#

  • Rank the factors: state policy > foreign capital > private and agricultural demand. Foreign capital was arguably as large in pure money terms, but it was a consequence of state policy, so it can't be the main reason on its own.
  • Final judgement: state policy was the main reason for growth across 1894–1914, decisively so before 1903 and less so after 1905. Change over time is the key point. By 1914 Russia was beginning to move from state-driven to market-driven growth, which suggests Gerschenkron's model describes the start of the period better than the end.

Essay 3: The limits of economic modernisation#

Question: "Russia's economic modernisation in the years 1894–1914 was fundamentally limited by the backwardness of agriculture." Assess the validity of this view.#

Line of argument: agricultural backwardness was the fundamental limit because it held back everything else: the domestic market, the tax base, labour and social stability. Dependence on foreign finance and the autocracy's political choices were also serious limits, but they were partly consequences of a poor peasant economy, or made worse by one.

Introduction#

  • Define: "fundamentally limited" means the root constraint that the other weaknesses grew out of. "Agricultural backwardness" means the commune, strip farming, low yields, land hunger and peasant poverty.
  • Context: Witte's model paid for industry with grain exports and taxes on peasants. Stolypin admitted the countryside was the problem when he called his reforms a "wager on the strong".
  • Judgement: the view is largely valid. Backward agriculture was the fundamental limit because it capped domestic demand, forced dependence on foreign capital and fed the social unrest of 1902–07. Financial dependence and political instability are serious secondary limits that should be weighed against it.

Paragraph 1: Agricultural backwardness (the fundamental limit)#

  • Point: a poor, unproductive peasantry restricted modernisation at its root.
  • Evidence:
    • More than 80% of people were peasants. The commune, strip farming and periodic redistribution of land discouraged investment.
    • Population rose from about 125m to about 170m, so land hunger grew and holdings were subdivided.
    • The 1891–92 famine, and redemption payments until 1907.
    • Stolypin: about 2–2.5m households applied to leave the commune, but only around 10% farmed consolidated holdings by 1914. Around 3m peasants resettled in Siberia, and a significant minority returned.
  • Analysis: spell out the chain of consequences, because that is what makes this factor fundamental:
    1. Demand: poor peasants couldn't buy manufactured goods, so industry relied on state orders instead of a mass consumer market.
    2. Revenue: the state taxed peasant consumption (vodka, sugar, kerosene) and exported grain to pay for industry, which kept peasants poor.
    3. Labour: many workers were peasants who still belonged to their commune and moved between village and factory, so the workforce stayed semi-skilled and unsettled.
    4. Stability: peasant risings in 1902 and 1905–07 disrupted the economy and frightened off investors.
  • Counter (Gregory): agriculture grew about 2% a year, faster than population. The record 1913 harvest and the rise of the kulaks show it was improving.
  • Mini-judgement: improvement was too slow and too narrow to remove the limit. That makes it the fundamental constraint.

Paragraph 2: Dependence on foreign capital and state finance (a serious limit)#

  • Point: modernisation was limited by how it was funded. It relied on foreign loans and investment that Russia didn't control.
  • Evidence:
    • Foreign investment rose to about 2,000m roubles by 1914. Russia carried one of the world's largest foreign debts, mostly owed to France.
    • Large amounts of revenue went on debt interest.
    • The 1899–1903 recession began when European credit tightened, and Russian industry contracted.
    • Profits flowed abroad to French and Belgian shareholders.
  • Analysis: this left the economy exposed to forces outside Russia: a European credit crunch hit Russian factories directly. Link it back to the main argument, though. Russia borrowed abroad because a poor peasant economy produced too little domestic savings. So this limit is partly a symptom of agricultural backwardness, which supports the view in the question.
  • Mini-judgement: a major limit, but a secondary one, because it comes from the first.

Paragraph 3: Political and social instability under the autocracy (a serious limit)#

  • Point: the tsarist system limited modernisation by blocking the political and social changes a modern economy needs. This is the strongest alternative to the view.
  • Evidence:
    • Nicholas II distrusted industrialisation and moved Witte out in 1903. Witte's own memoirs complain the Tsar didn't understand economics.
    • War with Japan (1904–05) and the 1905 revolution interrupted growth, and Baku oil output fell after the unrest there.
    • Industrial workers: harsh conditions and an 11.5-hour legal day (1897). Huge factories concentrated workers together (over 40% in plants of 1,000+), which made them easy to organise.
    • Zubatov's police-run unions (1901–03) backfired. The Lena Goldfields massacre (1912) set off strikes: about 725,000 strikers in 1912 and about 1.3m in the first half of 1914.
  • Analysis: modernisation created an urban working class the autocracy couldn't absorb, so the more the economy grew, the more unstable the regime became. This limit was political rather than agricultural. But much of the unrest was rural (1902, 1905–07), and many workers came straight from the villages, bringing peasant grievances with them.
  • Mini-judgement: the most serious challenge to the view. Political choices mattered in their own right, especially the decisions to go to war in 1904 and 1914.

Conclusion#

  • Weigh it up: financial dependence (P2) is best explained as a result of agricultural backwardness. Political instability (P3) is a real, separate limit. It was still made far more dangerous by an impoverished, land-hungry peasant majority.
  • Final judgement: the view is largely valid. Agricultural backwardness was the fundamental limit because it was the root cause behind the other weaknesses: a narrow market, foreign dependence and social unrest. The autocracy's political failures were the main aggravating factor. Stolypin's "twenty years of peace" shows both points: he knew agriculture was the foundation, and he knew politics would decide whether the reforms had time to work. In 1914 they did not.

How to turn a plan into a top-band essay#

  1. Answer the question in the first sentence of every paragraph. "Witte built railways" is narrative. "State railway building was the main driver of growth in the 1890s because…" is argument.
  2. Every statistic needs a "so what". Don't just write "coal output rose sixfold". Say what it proves about the question.
  3. Use change over time. All three questions cover 20 years. Contrasting 1894–1903 with 1906–14 is the quickest way to show the period-wide understanding examiners want.
  4. Link your paragraphs to each other. The strongest essays show how factors connect, for example "foreign capital came because of the gold standard" or "foreign borrowing was caused by low domestic savings". That is what makes a judgement substantiated rather than asserted.
  5. Write the judgement in the introduction and keep to it. Don't save your argument for the conclusion.

W.E.B. Du Bois

Event · 1865–1877

Reconstruction and the Civil War Amendments

Freedom, citizenship and the vote, then retreat.

What happened

After the Civil War, three amendments abolished slavery (Thirteenth, 1865), made freed people citizens with equal protection of the law (Fourteenth, 1868), and banned denying the vote on grounds of race (Fifteenth, 1870). The Freedmen's Bureau set up schools. Under federal protection, hundreds of Black men held office, including Hiram Revels, the first Black US Senator (1870).

White Southerners fought back with Black Codes and the violence of the Ku Klux Klan. Northern interest faded, and the Compromise of 1877 withdrew federal troops from the South. Reconstruction was over.

Fact file

  • Freedmen's Bureau: 1865.
  • Ku Klux Klan founded in Tennessee in 1865–66.
  • The Civil Rights Act of 1875 was struck down by the Supreme Court in 1883.
  • Around 2,000 Black men held public office during Reconstruction.

Timeline

  1. 1865Thirteenth Amendment; Freedmen's Bureau.
  2. 1868Fourteenth Amendment.
  3. 1870Fifteenth Amendment; Revels in the Senate.
  4. 1877Federal troops withdrawn.

The Supreme Court approves "separate but equal".

What happened

Homer Plessy, who was one-eighth Black, deliberately sat in a whites-only railway carriage in Louisiana to test the state's Separate Car Act. Event · April – August 1963

Birmingham and the March on Washington

Televised brutality and a mass march push Kennedy to act.

What happened

In Birmingham, Alabama, King and the SCLC launched "Project C" to confront segregation in America's most segregated city. When adults were jailed, children marched in the "Children's Crusade" in May. Police chief Bull Connor turned dogs and fire hoses on them, in front of TV cameras. On 11 June Kennedy announced a civil rights bill. Event · 1964–1965

The Civil Rights Act and the Voting Rights Act

Federal law ends legal segregation and protects the vote.

What happened

The Civil Rights Act (2 July 1964) banned segregation in public places and discrimination in employment, and created the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission. It passed after the longest filibuster in Senate history was broken. Voting remained the gap. After Freedom Summer (1964) and Selma's "Bloody Sunday" (7 March 1965), when marchers were beaten on the Edmund Pettus Bridge, the Voting Rights Act (6 August 1965) suspended literacy tests and sent federal examiners to register voters.

Fact file

  • The Senate filibuster lasted about two months before it was broken.
  • Twenty-fourth Amendment (1964) banned poll taxes in federal elections.
  • Black voter registration in Mississippi rose from about 7% in 1964 to about 60% in 1967.

Event · 1965–1970s

Black Power and the Black Panthers

A turn to pride, self-defence and community control.

What happened

The Watts riot in Los Angeles (August 1965) showed that Northern and Western cities faced problems the Southern movement had not solved: poverty, housing and police brutality. In June 1966 Stokely Carmichael of SNCC called for "Black Power". SNCC expelled its white members. In Oakland, Huey Newton and Bobby Seale founded the Black Panther Party (1966), which combined armed patrols of the police with community programmes such as free breakfasts. The FBI's COINTELPRO set out to destroy it.

Fact file

  • Watts: 34 dead. Detroit (1967): 43 dead.
  • The Kerner Commission (1968) warned the nation was "moving toward two societies, one black, one white".
  • Tommie Smith and John Carlos gave the Black Power salute at the 1968 Olympics.
  • Panther leader Fred Hampton was killed in a police raid in Chicago in 1969.
