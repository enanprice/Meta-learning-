Person · 1491–1547

Henry VIII

King of England from 1509; the man Wolsey served.

Who they were

The second son of Henry VII, Henry became heir when his brother Arthur died in 1502 and king at 17. He was tall, athletic, cultured and hungry for glory: he wanted to be a warrior king like Henry V and to win back the French crown. He disliked the routine of paperwork and was happy to let a capable servant do it, as long as the results matched his wishes.

That is the key to the Wolsey years. Henry set the goals: war, glory, money, and from 1527 an annulment. Wolsey found the means. When Wolsey failed to deliver the annulment, Henry let him fall.

Fact file

  • Became king on 21 April 1509, aged 17.
  • Married Catherine of Aragon, his brother's widow, in June 1509.
  • Executed his father's unpopular tax collectors, Empson and Dudley, in 1510.
  • Led his army in person in France in 1513.
  • Wrote the Assertio Septem Sacramentorum against Luther (1521) and was named Defender of the Faith.
  • Stood as a candidate in the imperial election of 1519.
  • Had one surviving child with Catherine by 1529: Princess Mary, born 1516.

Significance and connotations

What mentioning this in an essay signals, and the arguments it supports.

  • Who was in charge? The central debate of the period. One view (Pollard) is that Wolsey ruled as alter rex, the "other king". Another (Gwyn, Scarisbrick) is that Henry set policy and Wolsey carried it out. Henry's interventions, such as the 1513 war, the 1525 Amicable Grant climbdown and the annulment, are your evidence.
  • Glory over prudence. Henry's wish for martial glory explains why England went to war in 1512 and 1522 when it could not afford to.
  • Unpredictable loyalty. Henry dropped Empson and Dudley in 1510 and Wolsey in 1529. Ministers held power only while they were useful.

Person · c.1473–1530

Thomas Wolsey

Lord Chancellor, Archbishop of York, cardinal and papal legate; Henry's chief minister 1514–1529.

Who they were

The son of an Ipswich butcher, Wolsey went to Magdalen College, Oxford, and graduated at 15. He was a chaplain to Henry VII. Under Henry VIII he became royal almoner in 1509 and made his name organising the 1513 French campaign. He was hard-working, brilliant with detail, and willing to do the work Henry found dull.

His rise was spectacular: Archbishop of York (1514), cardinal (1515), Lord Chancellor (1515) and legate a latere (1518), with authority over the whole English Church. He ran justice, finance and foreign policy and lived like a prince at Hampton Court and York Place. His wealth and arrogance made enemies among the nobility. When he could not get Henry's annulment, they helped bring him down.

Fact file

  • Royal almoner (November 1509), which gave him access to the king; a councillor from 1511.
  • Organised supplies and transport for the 1513 invasion of France.
  • Bishop of Lincoln and Archbishop of York (1514); cardinal (September 1515).
  • Lord Chancellor from December 1515 to October 1529.
  • Legate a latere from 1518, and for life from 1524.
  • Pluralist: also held Bath and Wells, Durham and then Winchester, and was Abbot of St Albans.
  • His income is estimated at around £30,000 a year, more than most nobles.
  • Had two illegitimate children with Joan Larke.
  • Tried to become Pope in 1521 and 1523.
  • Founded Cardinal College, Oxford (later Christ Church) and a college in Ipswich.

Significance and connotations

What mentioning this in an essay signals, and the arguments it supports.

  • Alter rex or servant? Mentioning Wolsey's offices and wealth supports the "other king" view. His fall, and moments like the Amicable Grant, show his power depended entirely on Henry.
  • The over-mighty churchman. He made the abuses anticlericals attacked visible: pluralism, absenteeism, wealth and illegitimate children. That links his career to the coming Reformation.
  • Self-made man. His low birth explains noble resentment, and why he relied wholly on royal favour.
  • His dying words. "Had I but served God as diligently as I have done the king, He would not have given me over in my grey hairs." A good line for a conclusion on his fall.

Person · 1485–1536

Catherine of Aragon

Henry's first queen; daughter of Ferdinand and Isabella, aunt of Charles V.

Who they were

Catherine came to England in 1501 to marry Prince Arthur, who died five months later. A papal dispensation from Julius II allowed her to marry Henry, and they married in June 1509. She was popular, intelligent and politically capable. As regent in 1513 she organised the defence against Scotland that led to Flodden.

She had at least six pregnancies, but only one child survived: Mary, born 1516. By the mid-1520s she was past childbearing. When Henry sought an annulment she fought it, insisting her marriage to Arthur had never been consummated. Her nephew, Charles V, controlled the Pope after 1527.

Fact file

  • Married Arthur in November 1501; he died in April 1502.
  • Dispensation from Pope Julius II (1503) allowed her to marry Henry.
  • Regent during Henry's 1513 campaign; sent Henry a piece of James IV's bloodied coat after Flodden.
  • Only surviving child: Mary (born 18 February 1516).
  • At Blackfriars (June 1529) she knelt before Henry, appealed to him, and then walked out, refusing to recognise the court.

Significance and connotations

What mentioning this in an essay signals, and the arguments it supports.

  • The Spanish link. Her marriage tied England to Spain. That explains the alliance with Ferdinand in 1511–13, and why Charles V would never let the Pope annul it.
  • Succession anxiety. No male heir is the root of the Great Matter.

Person · c.1501–1536

Anne Boleyn

Henry's mistress-in-waiting from c.1526, and an enemy of Wolsey.

Who they were

Daughter of the diplomat Thomas Boleyn and niece of the Duke of Norfolk, Anne was educated at the French court and returned to England in 1522. Henry was pursuing her by 1526–27. She refused to become his mistress, which made marriage, and therefore an annulment, necessary. She had evangelical sympathies and showed Henry reformist books. By 1529 she blamed Wolsey for the delays and worked with her family and their allies to destroy him.

Fact file

  • Her sister Mary had been Henry's mistress.
  • Henry's love letters to her survive in the Vatican Library.
  • Brought Tyndale's Obedience of a Christian Man (1528), which argued kings should rule the Church, to Henry's attention.
  • Married Henry in January 1533.

Timeline

  1. 1522Returns from France.
  2. 1526–27Henry's courtship begins.

  4. 1533Marries Henry.

  • Love or heir? Her refusal to be a mistress raised the stakes of the annulment.

Person · 1457–1509

Henry VII

Henry VIII's father; the inheritance he left shaped 1509.

Who they were

Henry VII won the throne at Bosworth in 1485 and spent his reign making it secure. He married Elizabeth of York, defeated pretenders, and controlled the nobility through bonds and recognisances: legal promises to pay huge sums if they misbehaved. He avoided costly wars and left the Crown solvent, but his financial methods, enforced by Empson and Dudley, made him feared and unpopular by 1509.

Fact file

  • By one count, 46 of 62 noble families were under bonds at some point in his reign.
  • The Council Learned in the Law enforced his financial rights.
  • Left the Crown solvent and the dynasty secure, with an adult heir.
  • Arranged Arthur's marriage to Catherine (Treaty of Medina del Campo, 1489).

  1. 1485Bosworth.

Significance and connotations

Event · 1515–1529

Wolsey's Justice: Star Chamber and Chancery

Wolsey promises justice for all and uses the courts against the powerful.

What happened

As Lord Chancellor, Wolsey sat in the Court of Chancery and the Council's court in Star Chamber. In 1516 he announced the "new law of Star Chamber": impartial justice for rich and poor alike. He encouraged ordinary people to bring cases, and the courts were soon overwhelmed. He used Star Chamber to discipline powerful men. The Earl of Northumberland was imprisoned in 1516, and Sir Robert Sheffield, who had complained about Wolsey, was jailed in 1518. Overflow courts had to be set up in 1520.

Event · 1522–1523

Wolsey squeezes the country to pay for war with France.

What happened

Event · 1511–1515

The Hunne Case and the Standish Affair

Anticlerical crises that tested the Church's privileges.

What happened

Richard Hunne, a London merchant, refused to pay a mortuary fee (a payment to the priest) after his baby son died in 1511. He sued the clergy under praemunire. In return he was arrested for heresy and found hanged in the Bishop of London's Lollards' Tower in December 1514. A coroner's jury accused the bishop's chancellor of murder. London was outraged.

At the same time Henry Standish, a friar, defended an Act limiting "benefit of clergy", which allowed clerics to escape the king's courts. Called before Convocation for it, he appealed to the king. Henry declared that "kings of England in time past have never had any superior but God alone". Wolsey knelt to ask that the matter be referred to Rome, and it was quietly dropped.

Fact file

  • Hunne's body was tried for heresy after his death and burned.
  • The coroner's jury blamed Dr Horsey, the bishop's chancellor.
  • Henry's statement on Standish was made at Baynard's Castle, November 1515.

Event · 1511–1513

Henry's first war: a failure in Gascony, then glory in Flanders.

What happened

Pope Julius II formed the Holy League against France in 1511 with Spain and Venice. Henry joined in November 1511. Allied with Emperor Maximilian, he won the Battle of the Spurs at Guinegate (16 August), so called because the French cavalry fled so fast, and captured Thérouanne and Tournai.   • England joined the Holy League by the Treaty of Westminster, November 1511.
  • Dorset's 1512 army lost men to dysentery and mutinied.

  • Maximilian served under Henry for pay.
  • Tournai surrendered in late September 1513; Wolsey was named its bishop.
  • The wars of 1511–14 cost around £1 million, using up the reserves Henry VII had left.

Event · October 1518

A European peace with England at its centre.

What happened

Released in 1526, Francis immediately broke the Treaty of Madrid and formed the League of Cognac (May 1526) with Pope Clement VII, Venice, Florence and Milan against Charles. England backed it as "protector" without joining in full. In May 1527 Charles's unpaid army mutinied and sacked Rome. The Pope fled to the Castel Sant'Angelo and was left in Charles's power, just as Henry began to seek his annulment.

  • Sack of Rome: 6 May 1527, by mutinous imperial troops including German Lutheran soldiers.

Event · 1527–1528

The French Alliance and War with Charles V

England allies with France and declares war on its main trading partner.

What happened

Wolsey's Treaty of Westminster (April 1527) and his personal embassy to France, which produced the Treaty of Amiens (August 1527), bound England to France. He hoped French support would free the Pope. In January 1528 England and France declared war on Charles V. No English army fought, but trade with the Netherlands, where English cloth was sold, was disrupted. Cloth workers in Kent, Essex and the West Country were laid off and there was unrest. A truce was agreed in June 1528.

Event · 1527–1529

The King's Great Matter

Henry's campaign to annul his marriage to Catherine.

What happened

By 1527 Henry had no son, Catherine was past childbearing, and he was in love with Anne Boleyn. He argued his marriage broke the law of God in Leviticus 20:21: "If a man shall take his brother's wife... they shall be childless". That meant Julius II should never have granted a dispensation.

In May 1527 Wolsey held a secret court at York Place to examine the marriage, then abandoned it. The Sack of Rome left the Pope in Charles V's power. Missions to the Pope (Knight in 1527; Gardiner and Foxe in 1528) won a commission for Wolsey and Campeggio to hear the case in England, but not the binding power to decide it.

Fact file

  • Henry's argument rested on Leviticus; Catherine's defence rested on her marriage to Arthur never having been consummated.
  • Wolsey's secret court: May 1527.
  • Henry's secretary William Knight was sent to the Pope in 1527, partly behind Wolsey's back.
  • Wolsey's own embassy to France (summer 1527) aimed to win French help for the annulment.

Timeline

  1. 1526–27Henry pursues Anne Boleyn.
  2. May 1527Secret court; Sack of Rome.
  3. 1528Gardiner's mission; Campeggio sent.
  4. May–Jul 1529Blackfriars.

Significance and connotations

What mentioning this in an essay signals, and the arguments it supports.

  • Love, heir or conscience? Henry's motives are disputed: the need for a male heir, his passion for Anne, and a possibly genuine scruple about Leviticus.
  • Wolsey's impossible task. The annulment depended on a Pope controlled by Catherine's nephew.

Event · May–July 1529

The Blackfriars Legatine Court

Wolsey and Campeggio try the marriage case in London.

What happened

Event · October 1529 – November 1530

The Fall of Wolsey

Charged, stripped of office, pardoned, arrested, and dead.

What happened

In October 1529 Wolsey was charged with praemunire: exercising papal (legatine) authority in England, which harmed royal power, even though Henry had backed it. He surrendered the Great Seal to Norfolk and Suffolk and pleaded guilty. Thomas More replaced him as Lord Chancellor. The Reformation Parliament met in November, and the Lords drew up articles of complaint against him.

Henry pardoned him in February 1530 and let him keep York, his one diocese he had never visited. Wolsey went north and planned a grand enthronement. He also wrote to the French and Imperial ambassadors, hoping to recover power. In November 1530 the Earl of Northumberland arrested him for treason at Cawood. Sick on the journey south, he died at Leicester Abbey on 29 November 1530.

Fact file

  • Indicted for praemunire, 9 October 1529.
  • Surrendered the Great Seal on 17 October 1529.
  • Surrendered York Place to the king; Hampton Court had already passed to Henry.
  • Articles of complaint by the Lords (December 1529).
  • Arrested 4 November 1530 at Cawood Castle by Henry Percy, Earl of Northumberland, who as a young man had been barred by Wolsey from marrying Anne Boleyn.
  • Died 29 November 1530.

Timeline

  1. Jul 1529Blackfriars fails.
  2. Aug 1529Cambrai.
  3. Oct 1529Praemunire charge; Great Seal surrendered.
  4. Nov 1529Reformation Parliament.
  5. Feb 1530Pardoned.
  6. Nov 1530Arrested; dies.

Significance and connotations

What mentioning this in an essay signals, and the arguments it supports.

  • Many causes, one trigger. The annulment triggered his fall, but noble enmity, the Boleyn faction, failed finance (1525), diplomatic isolation (Cambrai) and anticlerical anger all left him without friends.
  • Henry's choice. In the end only Henry's favour mattered, which is the strongest evidence that Wolsey was never truly alter rex.

Person · 1566–1625

James I

King of Scotland from 1567 and of England 1603–1625.

Who they were

An experienced ruler and scholar, James believed in the divine right of kings and wrote about it. In England he faced a Crown that was short of money, and he made it worse with generous gifts to favourites, especially George Villiers, Duke of Buckingham. His relations with Parliament were often tense, over money, his prerogative and foreign policy, but they never broke down completely.

Fact file

  • Hampton Court Conference (1604) with Puritans; led to the King James Bible (1611).
  • The Great Contract (1610) would have swapped feudal dues for a fixed income; it failed.
  • The Addled Parliament (1614) passed no laws.
  • Parliament's Protestation (1621) asserted its right to free speech; James tore it out of the Commons Journal.
  • The Spanish Match, a planned marriage for Charles, collapsed in 1623.

  1. 1603English king.
  2. 1605Gunpowder Plot.
  3. 1610Great Contract fails.
  4. 1614Addled Parliament.
  5. 1621Protestation.
  6. 1625Dies.

Person · 1600–1649

Charles I

King 1625–1649; executed by his own subjects.

Who they were

Shy, formal and convinced of his divine right, Charles quarrelled with Parliament over war finance, Buckingham and religion, then ruled without it from 1629. His attempt to impose an English-style Prayer Book on Scotland in 1637 sparked a rebellion that forced him to recall Parliament in 1640. Mistrust between Charles and Parliament's leaders led to war in 1642.

Defeated in 1646, he kept negotiating with every side while plotting with the Scots. That brought the Second Civil War in 1648 and convinced the army he could never be trusted. He was tried and beheaded in January 1649.

Fact file

  • Married the Catholic French princess Henrietta Maria in 1625.
  • Forced Loan (1626–27); Petition of Right (1628).
  • Personal Rule 1629–40.
  • Attempted to arrest the Five Members, January 1642.
  • The Engagement with the Scots (December 1647) led to the Second Civil War.
  • Last word on the scaffold: "Remember".

Person · 1599–1658

Oliver Cromwell

Parliament's greatest general; Lord Protector 1653–1658.

Who they were

A Cambridgeshire gentleman and Puritan MP, Cromwell built a reputation as a cavalry commander, at Marston Moor (1644) and Naseby (1645). He helped create the New Model Army and became its most powerful figure. He signed Charles I's death warrant, conquered Ireland brutally (Drogheda and Wexford, 1649) and defeated the Scots at Dunbar and Worcester.

He dissolved the Rump in 1653 and became Lord Protector. He tried to build a godly, settled nation, but relied on the army, including the rule of the Major-Generals (1655–57). He refused the crown in 1657 and died in 1658.

Fact file

  • Lieutenant-General of Horse in the New Model Army.
  • Putney Debates (1647) with the Levellers.
  • Drogheda (1649): thousands killed after the town was stormed.
  • Refused the crown under the Humble Petition and Advice (1657).
  • Died on 3 September 1658; his body was dug up and "executed" in 1661.

Person · 1630–1685

Charles II

King 1660–1685; the "Merry Monarch" of the Restoration.

Who they were

Charles spent the 1650s in exile, returning in 1660 on the terms of the Declaration of Breda. Clever and pragmatic, he accepted that he had to work with Parliament but secretly leaned toward France and Catholicism. In the Secret Treaty of Dover (1670) he promised Louis XIV he would convert. The Popish Plot and the Exclusion Crisis (1678–81), an attempt to bar his Catholic brother James from the throne, created the first parties, Whigs and Tories. He ruled without Parliament from 1681 and converted on his deathbed.

Fact file

  • Declaration of Breda (April 1660).

Person · 1633–1701

James II

Catholic king 1685–1688; lost his throne to William of Orange.

Who they were

Charles II's brother, a Catholic convert, succeeded without trouble in 1685 and crushed Monmouth's rebellion. He then pushed to give Catholics equal rights: appointing Catholic officers, issuing Declarations of Indulgence, and prosecuting the Seven Bishops who refused to read them. The birth of a Catholic son in June 1688 meant a Catholic line. Leading nobles invited William of Orange, and James's support collapsed. He fled to France in December 1688.

Fact file

  • Monmouth's rebellion was defeated at Sedgemoor (1685); Judge Jeffreys' "Bloody Assizes" followed.
  • Declarations of Indulgence: 1687 and 1688.
  • The Seven Bishops were acquitted in June 1688.
  • Son James Francis Edward born in June 1688.
  • Defeated in Ireland at the Boyne (1690).

Timeline

  1. 1685King; Sedgemoor.
  2. 1687Declaration of Indulgence.
  3. 1688Seven Bishops; son born; William lands; James flees.
  4. 1690The Boyne.
  5. 1701Dies in France.

Significance and connotations

  • Self-destruction. He inherited a strong position and lost it in three years, which is the core of "responsible for his own downfall" questions.

Event · November 1605

The Gunpowder Plot

Catholic plotters try to blow up king and Parliament.

What happened

Robert Catesby and a small group of Catholics, disappointed that James had not eased anti-Catholic laws, placed 36 barrels of gunpowder under the House of Lords. An anonymous letter warned Lord Monteagle, and Guy Fawkes was caught on the night of 4–5 November. The plotters were killed or executed. An Oath of Allegiance was imposed on Catholics in 1606.

Fact file

  • Plot leader: Robert Catesby.
  • Monteagle letter passed to Robert Cecil.
  • Plotters executed in January 1606.

Timeline

  1. 1604Plot begins.
  2. 4–5 Nov 1605Fawkes arrested.
  3. 1606Executions; Oath of Allegiance.

Significance and connotations

What mentioning this in an essay signals, and the arguments it supports.

  • Anti-Catholicism. It fixed the link between Catholicism and treason in English minds for a century, and explains the fears about Henrietta Maria, Laud and James II.

Event · 1628

The Petition of Right

Parliament's statement of the subject's liberties.

What happened

After the Forced Loan and the jailing of those who refused to pay it (the Five Knights' Case, 1627), Parliament drew up the Petition of Right. It declared illegal any tax without Parliament's consent, imprisonment without cause shown, billeting soldiers on civilians, and martial law in peacetime. Charles accepted it to get war taxes, then ignored it. Disputes over customs duties (tonnage and poundage) and religion led to the dissolution of 1629.

Fact file

  • Five Knights' Case (1627) upheld imprisonment by royal command.
  • Three Resolutions (March 1629) were passed while the Speaker was held down in his chair.

Timeline

  1. 1626–27Forced Loan.
  2. 1628Petition of Right.
  3. Mar 1629Three Resolutions; dissolution.

Significance and connotations

What mentioning this in an essay signals, and the arguments it supports.

  • Constitutional principle. It shows Parliament defending law and property, not trying to seize power.

Event · 1629–1640

The Personal Rule

Eleven years without Parliament.

What happened

Charles made peace with France (1629) and Spain (1630) and raised money without Parliament. He revived old feudal rights: distraint of knighthood, forest fines and monopolies. Above all he used Ship Money, extended from coastal counties to the whole country in 1635. It brought in about £200,000 a year at first. John Hampden's refusal led to a court case in 1637–38 that the Crown won by just 7 votes to 5, and payment fell sharply. Laud's church reforms ran alongside.

Fact file

  • Ship Money collection was close to 90% early on and had collapsed by 1639.
  • "Thorough": a policy of efficient central rule associated with Wentworth and Laud.

Timeline

  1. 1629Parliament dissolved.
  2. 1635Ship Money extended inland.
  3. 1637–38Hampden's case.
  4. 1640Short Parliament ends the Personal Rule.

Significance and connotations

What mentioning this in an essay signals, and the arguments it supports.

  • "Eleven Years' Tyranny" or a workable system? Revisionists like Sharpe argue it was working until the Scottish crisis broke it.

Event · 1637–1640

The Scottish Crisis and the Bishops' Wars

Scotland rebels against Charles's Prayer Book.

What happened

In July 1637 the reading of a new Prayer Book in St Giles' Cathedral, Edinburgh, provoked a riot. The Scots signed a National Covenant (1638) to defend their Presbyterian church and abolished bishops. Charles's attempts to crush them in the First (1639) and Second (1640) Bishops' Wars failed. The Scots occupied Newcastle, and the Treaty of Ripon made Charles pay them £850 a day, which forced him to recall Parliament.

Fact file

  • Prayer Book riot: 23 July 1637.
  • Battle of Newburn (August 1640): the Scots defeat the English.

Timeline

  1. 1637Prayer Book riot.
  2. 1638National Covenant.
  3. 1639First Bishops' War.
  4. 1640Second Bishops' War; Ripon.

Event · 1640–1642

The Long Parliament and the Slide to War

Reform, mistrust, and the split into two sides.

What happened

In 1641 the Long Parliament, led by John Pym, dismantled the Personal Rule. It executed Strafford, abolished Star Chamber and the prerogative courts, declared Ship Money illegal, and passed a Triennial Act. Nearly everyone agreed on that. The split came over religion and control of the army. After the Irish rebellion (October 1641), the Grand Remonstrance listing Charles's misdeeds passed by only 159 to 148, and a royalist party formed. Charles's attempt to arrest the Five Members in January 1642 destroyed trust. Parliament's Militia Ordinance and the king's rejection of the Nineteen Propositions led to war.

Fact file

  • Triennial Act (February 1641).
  • Act against dissolving Parliament without its own consent (May 1641).
  • Star Chamber and High Commission abolished (July 1641).
  • Grand Remonstrance: 22 November 1641, 159–148.
  • Five Members: 4 January 1642.

Timeline

  1. Nov 1640Long Parliament meets.
  2. May 1641Strafford executed.
  3. Oct 1641Irish rebellion.
  4. Nov 1641Grand Remonstrance.
  5. Jan 1642Five Members.
  6. Jun 1642Nineteen Propositions.

Event · 1642–1648

The Civil Wars

Parliament defeats the king, twice.

What happened

The first battle, Edgehill (October 1642), was indecisive. Parliament gained the Scots as allies in 1643 (the Solemn League and Covenant) and won at Marston Moor in 1644. The Self-Denying Ordinance and the New Model Army under Fairfax, with Cromwell commanding the cavalry, delivered the decisive win at Naseby in June 1645. Charles surrendered to the Scots in 1646. A Second Civil War in 1648, with Scottish support for the king, was crushed at Preston.

Fact file

  • Parliament held London, the navy and most of the richer south-east.
  • New Model Army created in 1645.
  • Naseby captured the king's letters, which revealed his plans to bring in foreign troops.

Timeline

  1. 1642Edgehill.
  2. 1643Solemn League and Covenant.
  3. 1644Marston Moor.
  4. 1645Naseby.
  5. 1646Charles surrenders.
  6. 1648Preston.

Significance and connotations

  • Why Parliament won. Resources (London and the navy), the Scots, and the New Model Army.
  • The army as a political force. By 1647 the army had its own agenda, which led to the regicide.

Event · December 1648 – January 1649

The Regicide

The army purges Parliament and tries the king.

What happened

With MPs still negotiating with Charles after the Second Civil War, Colonel Pride's soldiers barred about 140 of them from the Commons on 6 December 1648. The remaining "Rump" set up a High Court of Justice. Charles refused to recognise its authority. 59 commissioners signed the death warrant, and he was beheaded outside the Banqueting House in Whitehall on 30 January 1649. Monarchy and the House of Lords were abolished in March 1649.

Fact file

  • Pride's Purge: 6 December 1648.
  • 59 signatures on the death warrant.
  • Charles wore two shirts so he would not shiver and appear afraid.

Timeline

  1. Dec 1648Pride's Purge.
  2. Jan 1649Trial; execution.
  3. Mar 1649Monarchy abolished.

Significance and connotations

What mentioning this in an essay signals, and the arguments it supports.

  • Revolution by a minority. A small army-backed group killed the king, not the nation.
  • "That man of blood". The army's language shows they believed God had judged Charles.

Event · 1653–1659

The Protectorate

Cromwell rules as Lord Protector.

What happened

After dissolving the Rump (April 1653) and the failure of the nominated "Barebones" Parliament, Cromwell became Lord Protector under the Instrument of Government, a written constitution. After Penruddock's royalist rising in 1655 he divided England under the Major-Generals, who enforced order and godly reform. They were deeply unpopular and ended in 1657. The Humble Petition and Advice offered him the crown, which he refused. His son Richard succeeded in 1658 but fell in 1659.

Fact file

  • Instrument of Government: December 1653.
  • Major-Generals: 1655–57.
  • Richard Cromwell resigned in May 1659.
  • General Monck marched from Scotland in January 1660.

Timeline

  1. 1653Protectorate begins.
  2. 1655Major-Generals.
  3. 1657Humble Petition.
  4. 1658Oliver dies.
  5. 1659Richard falls.

Significance and connotations

What mentioning this in an essay signals, and the arguments it supports.

  • The search for settlement. Each experiment shows the difficulty of ruling without a king, or without the army.

Event · 1660

The Restoration

Charles II returns to the throne.

What happened

With the republic collapsing, General Monck marched south, recalled the MPs purged in 1648, and a Convention Parliament invited Charles back. His Declaration of Breda promised a pardon, religious tolerance and the settlement of land claims, all subject to Parliament. He entered London on 29 May 1660. The Act of Indemnity and Oblivion pardoned most people, but surviving regicides were executed.

Fact file

  • Declaration of Breda: April 1660.
  • Prerogative courts were not restored.
  • The Cavalier Parliament (1661–79) passed the Clarendon Code.

Timeline

  1. Jan 1660Monck marches south.
  2. Apr 1660Breda; Convention.
  3. May 1660Charles returns.

Significance and connotations

What mentioning this in an essay signals, and the arguments it supports.

  • What had changed? The king returned, but the reforms of 1641 stood. A monarchy restored with limits.

Event · 1688–1689

The Glorious Revolution

William and Mary replace James II on Parliament's terms.

What happened

Seven leading figures (the "Immortal Seven") invited William of Orange, husband of James's Protestant daughter Mary. William landed at Torbay on 5 November 1688. James's army and officials deserted him and he fled. A Convention Parliament declared that James had abdicated, offered the crown to William and Mary with a Declaration of Rights (February 1689), and passed the Bill of Rights (December 1689). There was serious bloodshed in Scotland and Ireland.

Fact file

  • Bill of Rights: no suspending laws, no standing army in peacetime, and no taxation without Parliament's consent.
  • Toleration Act (1689) for Protestant dissenters.
  • Annual Mutiny Acts and grants of money made yearly Parliaments necessary.
  • Triennial Act (1694); Act of Settlement (1701).

Timeline

  1. Jun 1688Invitation to William.
  2. Nov 1688William lands.
  3. Dec 1688James flees.
  4. Feb 1689William and Mary take the crown.
  5. Dec 1689Bill of Rights.

Significance and connotations

What mentioning this in an essay signals, and the arguments it supports.

  • Revolution or restoration of old rights? Whig historians saw the birth of parliamentary government; others stress its conservatism and William's own strong powers.

Person · 1868–1918

Nicholas II

Tsar of Russia, 1894–1917, and the last Romanov ruler.

Who they were

Nicholas inherited the throne at 26 and a firm belief, drilled into him by his father and his tutor Pobedonostsev, that God had given him unlimited power to hold in trust for his heir. He was dutiful and devoted to his family, but indecisive, poorly informed about his own country, and suspicious of ministers who were cleverer than him.

He survived 1905 by making concessions he then clawed back. In 1915 he took personal command of the army, which tied him directly to every defeat and left the government in Petrograd to his wife. When the Petrograd garrison mutinied in February 1917, his generals told him to abdicate, and he did.

Fact file

  • Called the hopes of reformers "senseless dreams" in January 1895, months into his reign.
  • His coronation celebrations in 1896 included the Khodynka Field crush, in which over 1,300 died. He attended a ball that evening.
  • Granted the October Manifesto (1905) under pressure, then issued the Fundamental Laws (1906) restating his "supreme autocratic power".
  • Dissolved the first two Dumas within months.
  • Took command of the army at Mogilev in September 1915, 800 km from Petrograd.
  • Abdicated for himself and his son on 2 March 1917. His brother Michael refused the throne the next day.
  • Shot with his family by the Bolsheviks at Ekaterinburg in July 1918.

Person · 1862–1911

Pyotr Stolypin

Prime Minister 1906–11: repression and land reform.

Who they were

A tough provincial governor, Stolypin became Interior Minister and then Prime Minister in 1906. His policy was "pacification first, then reform". Field courts-martial executed thousands of rebels; the noose became known as "Stolypin's necktie".

His land reforms let peasants leave the commune and consolidate their strips into private farms. He hoped to create a class of prosperous, loyal peasant farmers, a "wager on the strong". He said he needed twenty years of peace. He was shot in a Kiev theatre in September 1911.

Fact file

  • Field courts-martial (1906–07) executed over 1,000 people, with thousands more in later years.
  • Decree of 9 November 1906 allowed peasants to leave the commune.
  • By 1914 about 2–2.5 million households had applied to leave, but only around 10% of peasant households farmed fully consolidated holdings.
  • About 3 million peasants resettled in Siberia between 1906 and 1914.
  • Dissolved the Second Duma in June 1907 and changed the electoral law to favour landowners: the "Stolypin coup".
  • Assassinated by Dmitri Bogrov, a revolutionary who was also a police informer.

Event · 1906–1917

The Fundamental Laws and the Dumas

Russia's first parliament, granted and then hollowed out.

What happened

Days before the First Duma met, the Fundamental Laws (April 1906) declared that "supreme autocratic power" still belonged to the Tsar. He appointed ministers, controlled foreign policy and the army, and could rule by decree when the Duma was not sitting (Article 87).

The first two Dumas were radical and were dissolved within months. Stolypin then changed the electoral law, and the Third Duma (1907–12) was dominated by conservatives and ran its full term. The Fourth (1912–17) grew more critical, formed the Progressive Bloc in 1915, and its leaders formed the Provisional Government in 1917.

Fact file

  • First Duma: April–July 1906. Kadet majority; dissolved. The Vyborg Manifesto protest followed.
  • Second Duma: February–June 1907; dissolved.
  • June 1907 electoral law gave landowners far more weight than peasants and workers.
  • Third Duma 1907–12: the most cooperative.
  • Fourth Duma 1912–17: Progressive Bloc formed in August 1915.

Timeline

  1. Apr 1906Fundamental Laws; First Duma.
  2. 1907Second Duma dissolved; new electoral law.
  3. 1907–12Third Duma.
  4. 1912–17Fourth Duma.
  5. Feb 1917Duma committee becomes the Provisional Government.

What mentioning this in an essay signals, and the arguments it supports.

Event · 1914–1917

The First World War

Total war breaks the army, the economy and the monarchy.

What happened

Russia entered the war in July 1914 amid patriotic enthusiasm. Defeat at Tannenberg in August 1914 and the Great Retreat of 1915 cost millions of men and huge territory. Brusilov's offensive in 1916 succeeded at first, then stalled at enormous cost.

At home, conscription took peasants from the land, the railways could not carry food to the cities, and the government printed money. Prices rose roughly fourfold. Petrograd went short of bread and fuel in the cold winter of 1916–17.

Fact file

  • Tannenberg (August 1914): around 30,000 killed or wounded and about 90,000 captured.
  • Around 15 million men were mobilised during the war.
  • Great Retreat (1915): Poland and much of the western borderlands lost.
  • Brusilov Offensive (June 1916): early success, about a million Russian casualties.
  • Prices roughly quadrupled between 1914 and early 1917.

Timeline

  1. Jul 1914War declared.
  2. Aug 1914Tannenberg.
  3. 1915Great Retreat; Nicholas takes command.
  4. Jun 1916Brusilov Offensive.
  5. Winter 1916–17Food and fuel crisis in Petrograd.

Event · 1918–1921

The Civil War

Reds against Whites, Greens and foreign intervention.

What happened

The war spread after the Czech Legion revolted in May 1918. The Whites were a mix of monarchists, liberals and some socialists, led by generals such as Kolchak in Siberia, Denikin in the south and Yudenich near Petrograd. Britain, France, the USA and Japan sent troops, though in small numbers. Peasant "Green" armies fought both sides.

The Reds held the central heartland, with Moscow, Petrograd, most industry and the railway hub. They had one leadership and one aim. The Whites were spread around the edges, divided, and associated with the return of the landlords. By the end of 1920 the main White armies were beaten. War with Poland ended with the Treaty of Riga in 1921.

Fact file

  • Kolchak's advance from Siberia stopped in 1919; he was executed in 1920.
  • Denikin reached Orel, under 400 km from Moscow, in October 1919.
  • Yudenich reached the outskirts of Petrograd in October 1919.
  • Deaths from fighting, terror, disease and famine are estimated at 7–10 million.

The Russian Economy, 1894–1914

Three full essay plans, a timeline, a statistics bank and historians.

OverviewNotesEssays

Three plans on three different questions. Each takes its own line of argument, so don't merge them into one essay. Every plan uses the same shape:

  • Introduction: define the key terms, set the context and state your judgement up front.
  • Three paragraphs: each one opens with a point that answers the question, gives specific evidence, explains how the evidence proves the point, deals with the counter-argument, and ends with a mini-judgement tied back to the question.
  • Conclusion: weigh the factors against each other and give your final judgement. Don't summarise.

About the numbers. Russian statistics from this period are disputed, and textbooks give slightly different figures (coal output in 1913 appears as anything from 35 to 36 million tonnes, for example). The figures below are the ones most often used in A-level textbooks. Before an exam, check them against your own textbook and use its versions. Examiners reward accurate, confident use of evidence, and they mark down figures that look invented.

Essay 1: The extent of change#

Question: "The Russian economy was transformed in the years 1894–1914." Assess the validity of this view.#

Line of argument: Russia's industrial economy was transformed in scale. The structure of the economy as a whole was not: it stayed mostly agrarian, mostly peasant, and dependent on the state and foreign money. So "transformed" is valid for industry and invalid for the economy as a whole.

Introduction#

  • Define "transformed": a fundamental change in the structure of the economy, meaning what most people did for a living, where wealth came from and how production was organised. Rapid growth alone doesn't count.
  • Context: in 1894 Russia was a great power with a backward economy. The 1891–92 famine had just shown how vulnerable it was. Witte (Finance Minister 1892–1903) and later Stolypin (1906–11) tried to modernise it from above.
  • Judgement: heavy industry and transport changed dramatically. The economy as a whole was not transformed, because agriculture, the sector that employed more than 80% of the population, barely changed, and industrial growth depended on the state, foreign capital and peace. The best word for 1894–1914 is uneven rather than transformed.

Paragraph 1: Industry and infrastructure were transformed (for the view)#

  • Point: in heavy industry and transport the change was large enough to call a transformation.
  • Evidence:
    • Coal rose from 5.9m tonnes (1890) to 35.4m (1913), roughly six times over. Pig iron rose about fivefold, from 0.9m to 4.6m tonnes.
    • Industrial growth averaged about 8% a year in the 1890s and about 6% in 1908–13.
    • Track grew from about 30,600 km to about 70,200 km. The Trans-Siberian, begun in 1891, linked European Russia to the Pacific.
    • New industrial regions appeared: the Donbass for coal and iron, Baku for oil (the world's largest producer around 1900), and St Petersburg and Moscow for engineering and textiles.
    • Russia was roughly the fifth-largest industrial power by 1913.
  • Analysis: this was qualitative change as well as more output. Russia built a modern capital goods sector (coal, iron, steel, rail) almost from nothing in a single generation. Railways tied the national market together and opened up Siberian grain and Donbass coal. That is structural change in industry.
  • Counter: growth started from a very low base, so high percentages flatter the story. Per head, Russia still produced far less coal and iron than Britain, Germany or the USA.
  • Mini-judgement: within industry the view holds. Industry, though, was the smaller part of the economy.

Paragraph 2: Agriculture, the core of the economy, was not transformed (against the view)#

  • Point: the sector that employed most Russians changed only at the margins, which undermines the claim that "the Russian economy" was transformed.
  • Evidence:
    • More than 80% of the population were peasants (1897 census). The factory workforce was only about 3m out of about 170m by 1914.
    • Strip farming, the three-field system and the mir (commune) dominated. Yields stayed well below Western European levels.
    • Stolypin's 1906 decree: about 2–2.5m households applied to leave the commune, but only around 10% farmed consolidated holdings by 1914. Stolypin himself said he needed "twenty years of peace". He got about five before he was assassinated in 1911.
    • Population grew from about 125m to about 170m, so land hunger got worse.
  • Analysis: this is the strongest case against "transformed". Most Russians lived much as their parents had in 1894. Industry had been bolted onto a peasant economy without changing it. Witte's model actually depended on agriculture not changing: grain exports (about half of export earnings) and indirect taxes on peasant goods such as vodka, sugar and kerosene paid for industrialisation. Vyshnegradsky's line "we may go hungry, but we will export" sums it up.
  • Counter (Gregory): agricultural output grew about 2% a year, faster than population. The record harvest of 1913 and the end of redemption payments (1907) left better-off peasants (the kulaks) more prosperous.
  • Mini-judgement: there was real but limited improvement, and nothing like a transformation of the economy's foundations.

Paragraph 3: The way the economy worked stayed dependent (mostly against, with a caveat)#

  • Point: even where output changed, the motor of the economy didn't. Growth relied on the state and foreign capital rather than a self-sustaining domestic market. That is a sign of incomplete transformation.
  • Evidence:
    • Foreign investment rose from about 200m roubles (1890) to about 900m (1900) and about 2,000m (1914). The gold standard (1897) was adopted largely to attract it, mostly French loans.
    • The state was the main customer for rail and iron. When state orders and foreign credit dried up in the 1899–1903 recession, many firms collapsed.
    • Cartels and syndicates (Prodamet 1902, Produgol 1906) controlled prices rather than competing.
    • The caveat: after 1908 the boom was driven more by private Russian banks and domestic demand, for example consumer goods and agricultural machinery for richer peasants.
  • Analysis: an economy whose growth switches off when the state stops spending or Paris stops lending hasn't been transformed into a modern market economy. It has been artificially accelerated. The 1908–13 boom is the best evidence that it was starting to change, but six years isn't long enough to call it complete.
  • Mini-judgement: the 1908–13 shift shows transformation beginning, not finished.

Conclusion#

  • Weigh it up: the transformation of industry (P1) is real, but it was concentrated in a small modern sector employing a few million people. Agriculture (P2) carries more weight in the judgement because it was most of the economy and most of the people.
  • Final judgement: the view is valid for industry and infrastructure and invalid for the economy as a whole. Russia in 1914 had a modern industrial sector inside a traditional peasant economy. That is a dual economy, and dual economies are by definition not transformed. The social strain this produced (the 1912–14 strike wave and continuing land hunger) shows the change was destabilising rather than complete.

Essay 2: The fundamental cause of economic development#

Question: How far was state policy the main reason for Russian economic growth in the years 1894–1914?#

Line of argument: state policy was the main driver in 1894–1903, as Gerschenkron's model predicts. After 1905 its role shrank: foreign capital, private enterprise and better harvests did more of the work. The state stayed the most important single factor across the whole period because it created the conditions the other factors relied on, but it became less dominant over time.

Introduction#

  • Define: "state policy" means government action: tariffs, railway building, state orders, currency and tax policy, and the Stolypin land reforms. "Economic growth" means rising output in industry and agriculture.
  • Context: Russia had little private capital, a small middle class and a mostly illiterate peasantry (about 21% literacy in 1897). Gerschenkron argues that in that situation the state must replace the missing entrepreneurs.
  • Judgement: state policy was the main reason overall, but its importance changed over the period. It dominated under Witte. After 1905 its direct role faded, and growth came more from foreign capital, private banks and agriculture, all of which still relied on conditions the state had created.

Paragraph 1: State policy under Witte, 1894–1903 (main factor)#

  • Point: the 1890s boom was deliberately engineered by the state.
  • Evidence:
    • The 1891 Mendeleev tariff kept out foreign manufactures and made foreign firms build factories inside Russia.
    • State railway building: the Trans-Siberian, and track up from about 30,600 km to about 53,200 km by 1900. The state ordered the rails, locomotives and coal.
    • The 1897 gold standard stabilised the rouble.
    • Indirect taxes and the vodka monopoly (from 1894) funded investment. The vodka monopoly became one of the state's largest single sources of revenue.
    • Result: about 8% annual industrial growth. Coal went from 5.9m tonnes (1890) to 16.1m (1900).
  • Analysis: the state did more than help growth. It created demand (rail orders), protected supply (tariffs), and financed it (taxes and loans). Without that, private actors would not have invested in a country this risky. Witte's 1899 memorandum to Nicholas II explicitly argued industrialisation was needed so Russia would not become a "colony" of the West.
  • Counter: state-led growth was fragile. The 1899–1903 recession showed it could not sustain itself, and it squeezed the peasantry (the 1902 Poltava and Kharkov risings).
  • Mini-judgement: for the first decade, state policy was clearly the main reason.

Paragraph 2: Foreign capital (strong rival factor, but dependent on the state)#

  • Point: the money that actually built Russian industry was largely foreign, which challenges the idea that the state was the "main" reason.
  • Evidence:
    • Foreign investment went from about 200m to about 900m roubles in 1890–1900, and about 2,000m by 1914.
    • Foreign capital is often estimated at around a third or more of all industrial investment, and far higher in mining and metallurgy. French and Belgian capital dominated the Donbass, and the Nobels and Rothschilds were in Baku oil.
    • French state loans after the Franco-Russian alliance (1894) also funded the railways.
  • Analysis: foreign capital was a necessary condition, since Russia did not have the savings to fund growth on this scale. But it followed state policy. Investors came because of the gold standard, the tariff walls and the guaranteed state contracts. So this factor partly supports the state's case: the state was the reason the foreign money arrived.
  • Mini-judgement: this is the most important source of growth, but it was dependent on state policy rather than independent of it.

Paragraph 3: After 1905, private enterprise, agriculture and the market (state role declining)#

  • Point: the 1908–13 boom had a different engine. Private and market forces mattered more and direct state spending mattered less.
  • Evidence:
    • Industrial growth was about 6% a year in 1908–13, led by Russian joint-stock banks, consumer goods and agricultural machinery rather than state rail orders.
    • Agriculture: good harvests (a record in 1913), the end of redemption payments (1907), and the Peasant Land Bank helping richer peasants buy land. These raised rural demand for manufactured goods.
    • Kokovtsov balanced budgets and built up gold reserves. That was a more cautious state that stabilised the economy rather than driving it.
    • Stolypin's reforms were state policy, but their effect was limited (around 10% consolidated holdings by 1914). Good harvests probably did more than the reforms did.
  • Analysis: this is the strongest case against "main reason". By 1914 the economy was starting to grow from domestic demand, which is what Gregory emphasises. Even so, the state still set the framework: tariffs, the currency, railways already built, and the Land Bank.
  • Mini-judgement: after 1905 the state was an enabler more than an engine.

Conclusion#

  • Rank the factors: state policy > foreign capital > private and agricultural demand. Foreign capital was arguably as large in pure money terms, but it was a consequence of state policy, so it can't be the main reason on its own.
  • Final judgement: state policy was the main reason for growth across 1894–1914, decisively so before 1903 and less so after 1905. Change over time is the key point. By 1914 Russia was beginning to move from state-driven to market-driven growth, which suggests Gerschenkron's model describes the start of the period better than the end.

Essay 3: The limits of economic modernisation#

Question: "Russia's economic modernisation in the years 1894–1914 was fundamentally limited by the backwardness of agriculture." Assess the validity of this view.#

Line of argument: agricultural backwardness was the fundamental limit because it held back everything else: the domestic market, the tax base, labour and social stability. Dependence on foreign finance and the autocracy's political choices were also serious limits, but they were partly consequences of a poor peasant economy, or made worse by one.

Introduction#

  • Define: "fundamentally limited" means the root constraint that the other weaknesses grew out of. "Agricultural backwardness" means the commune, strip farming, low yields, land hunger and peasant poverty.
  • Context: Witte's model paid for industry with grain exports and taxes on peasants. Stolypin admitted the countryside was the problem when he called his reforms a "wager on the strong".
  • Judgement: the view is largely valid. Backward agriculture was the fundamental limit because it capped domestic demand, forced dependence on foreign capital and fed the social unrest of 1902–07. Financial dependence and political instability are serious secondary limits that should be weighed against it.

Paragraph 1: Agricultural backwardness (the fundamental limit)#

  • Point: a poor, unproductive peasantry restricted modernisation at its root.
  • Evidence:
    • More than 80% of people were peasants. The commune, strip farming and periodic redistribution of land discouraged investment.
    • Population rose from about 125m to about 170m, so land hunger grew and holdings were subdivided.
    • The 1891–92 famine, and redemption payments until 1907.
    • Stolypin: about 2–2.5m households applied to leave the commune, but only around 10% farmed consolidated holdings by 1914. Around 3m peasants resettled in Siberia, and a significant minority returned.
  • Analysis: spell out the chain of consequences, because that is what makes this factor fundamental:
    1. Demand: poor peasants couldn't buy manufactured goods, so industry relied on state orders instead of a mass consumer market.
    2. Revenue: the state taxed peasant consumption (vodka, sugar, kerosene) and exported grain to pay for industry, which kept peasants poor.
    3. Labour: many workers were peasants who still belonged to their commune and moved between village and factory, so the workforce stayed semi-skilled and unsettled.
    4. Stability: peasant risings in 1902 and 1905–07 disrupted the economy and frightened off investors.
  • Counter (Gregory): agriculture grew about 2% a year, faster than population. The record 1913 harvest and the rise of the kulaks show it was improving.
  • Mini-judgement: improvement was too slow and too narrow to remove the limit. That makes it the fundamental constraint.

Paragraph 2: Dependence on foreign capital and state finance (a serious limit)#

  • Point: modernisation was limited by how it was funded. It relied on foreign loans and investment that Russia didn't control.
  • Evidence:
    • Foreign investment rose to about 2,000m roubles by 1914. Russia carried one of the world's largest foreign debts, mostly owed to France.
    • Large amounts of revenue went on debt interest.
    • The 1899–1903 recession began when European credit tightened, and Russian industry contracted.
    • Profits flowed abroad to French and Belgian shareholders.
  • Analysis: this left the economy exposed to forces outside Russia: a European credit crunch hit Russian factories directly. Link it back to the main argument, though. Russia borrowed abroad because a poor peasant economy produced too little domestic savings. So this limit is partly a symptom of agricultural backwardness, which supports the view in the question.
  • Mini-judgement: a major limit, but a secondary one, because it comes from the first.

Paragraph 3: Political and social instability under the autocracy (a serious limit)#

  • Point: the tsarist system limited modernisation by blocking the political and social changes a modern economy needs. This is the strongest alternative to the view.
  • Evidence:
    • Nicholas II distrusted industrialisation and moved Witte out in 1903. Witte's own memoirs complain the Tsar didn't understand economics.
    • War with Japan (1904–05) and the 1905 revolution interrupted growth, and Baku oil output fell after the unrest there.
    • Industrial workers: harsh conditions and an 11.5-hour legal day (1897). Huge factories concentrated workers together (over 40% in plants of 1,000+), which made them easy to organise.
    • Zubatov's police-run unions (1901–03) backfired. The Lena Goldfields massacre (1912) set off strikes: about 725,000 strikers in 1912 and about 1.3m in the first half of 1914.
  • Analysis: modernisation created an urban working class the autocracy couldn't absorb, so the more the economy grew, the more unstable the regime became. This limit was political rather than agricultural. But much of the unrest was rural (1902, 1905–07), and many workers came straight from the villages, bringing peasant grievances with them.
  • Mini-judgement: the most serious challenge to the view. Political choices mattered in their own right, especially the decisions to go to war in 1904 and 1914.

Conclusion#

  • Weigh it up: financial dependence (P2) is best explained as a result of agricultural backwardness. Political instability (P3) is a real, separate limit. It was still made far more dangerous by an impoverished, land-hungry peasant majority.
  • Final judgement: the view is largely valid. Agricultural backwardness was the fundamental limit because it was the root cause behind the other weaknesses: a narrow market, foreign dependence and social unrest. The autocracy's political failures were the main aggravating factor. Stolypin's "twenty years of peace" shows both points: he knew agriculture was the foundation, and he knew politics would decide whether the reforms had time to work. In 1914 they did not.

How to turn a plan into a top-band essay#

  1. Answer the question in the first sentence of every paragraph. "Witte built railways" is narrative. "State railway building was the main driver of growth in the 1890s because…" is argument.
  2. Every statistic needs a "so what". Don't just write "coal output rose sixfold". Say what it proves about the question.
  3. Use change over time. All three questions cover 20 years. Contrasting 1894–1903 with 1906–14 is the quickest way to show the period-wide understanding examiners want.
  4. Link your paragraphs to each other. The strongest essays show how factors connect, for example "foreign capital came because of the gold standard" or "foreign borrowing was caused by low domestic savings". That is what makes a judgement substantiated rather than asserted.
  5. Write the judgement in the introduction and keep to it. Don't save your argument for the conclusion.

Person · b. 1961

Barack Obama

First African American President, elected 2008.

Who they were

Born in Hawaii to a Kenyan father and a white American mother, Obama worked as a community organiser in Chicago and then as a law lecturer, and became a US Senator for Illinois in 2005. He won the 2008 election against John McCain with about 53% of the popular vote and 365 electoral votes. He took office in January 2009.

Fact file

  • Won about 95% of the Black vote in 2008.
  • Earlier pioneers: Shirley Chisholm's 1972 run, Jesse Jackson's 1984 and 1988 campaigns, and Douglas Wilder, the first elected Black governor (Virginia, 1989).
  • Elected while big gaps in Black wealth, income, imprisonment and school segregation remained.

Event · 1865–1877

Reconstruction and the Civil War Amendments

Freedom, citizenship and the vote, then retreat.

What happened

After the Civil War, three amendments abolished slavery (Thirteenth, 1865), made freed people citizens with equal protection of the law (Fourteenth, 1868), and banned denying the vote on grounds of race (Fifteenth, 1870). The Freedmen's Bureau set up schools. Under federal protection, hundreds of Black men held office, including Hiram Revels, the first Black US Senator (1870).

White Southerners fought back with Black Codes and the violence of the Ku Klux Klan. Northern interest faded, and the Compromise of 1877 withdrew federal troops from the South. Reconstruction was over.

Fact file

  • Freedmen's Bureau: 1865.
  • Ku Klux Klan founded in Tennessee in 1865–66.
  • The Civil Rights Act of 1875 was struck down by the Supreme Court in 1883.
  • Around 2,000 Black men held public office during Reconstruction.

Timeline

  1. 1865Thirteenth Amendment; Freedmen's Bureau.
  2. 1868Fourteenth Amendment.
  3. 1870Fifteenth Amendment; Revels in the Senate.
  4. 1877Federal troops withdrawn.

Significance and connotations

What mentioning this in an essay signals, and the arguments it supports.

  • Rights on paper. The amendments became the legal basis for the 20th-century movement, especially the Fourteenth in Brown.
  • False dawn. Reconstruction shows how quickly rights could be lost without federal will to enforce them.

Event · 1896

Plessy v Ferguson and Jim Crow

The Supreme Court approves "separate but equal".

What happened

Homer Plessy, who was one-eighth Black, deliberately sat in a whites-only railway carriage in Louisiana to test the state's Separate Car Act. The Supreme Court ruled 7–1 that segregation was legal as long as facilities were equal. Justice Harlan dissented: "Our Constitution is color-blind." Jim Crow segregation spread across the South. Literacy tests, poll taxes and violence stripped Black men of the vote.

Fact file

  • Registered Black voters in Louisiana fell from about 130,000 in 1896 to about 1,300 in 1904.
  • Mississippi's 1890 constitution led the way in disenfranchisement.
  • Over 3,400 African Americans were lynched between 1882 and 1968.

Timeline

  1. 1890Louisiana Separate Car Act; Mississippi constitution.
  2. 1892Plessy arrested.
  3. 1896Supreme Court ruling.

Significance and connotations

What mentioning this in an essay signals, and the arguments it supports.

  • The courts as obstacle. Before 1938 the Supreme Court did more to entrench segregation than end it, which matters for questions about the courts as a driver of change.

Event · 17 May 1954

Brown v Board of Education

The Supreme Court rules school segregation unconstitutional.

What happened

Combining five cases, including that of Linda Brown in Topeka, Kansas, the Supreme Court under Chief Justice Earl Warren ruled unanimously that "separate educational facilities are inherently unequal", overturning Plessy for schools. Brown II (1955) ordered desegregation "with all deliberate speed", a phrase the South used to delay. Southern states launched "massive resistance".

Fact file

  • Unanimous 9–0 decision.
  • Southern Manifesto (1956): 101 members of Congress pledged to resist.
  • By 1964 only about 1–2% of Black children in the Deep South attended integrated schools.

Timeline

  1. 1954Brown.
  2. 1955Brown II.
  3. 1956Southern Manifesto.
  4. 1957Little Rock.

Significance and connotations

What mentioning this in an essay signals, and the arguments it supports.

  • Landmark vs limits. A huge legal and symbolic victory, but little change on the ground for a decade. It inspired protest and also provoked white backlash.

Event · December 1955 – December 1956

The Montgomery Bus Boycott

A year-long boycott ends bus segregation and launches King.

What happened

On 1 December 1955 Rosa Parks, an experienced NAACP activist, refused to give up her seat to a white passenger and was arrested. Local leaders, led by E.D. Nixon and Jo Ann Robinson's Women's Political Council, organised a boycott, and the new Montgomery Improvement Association chose King as its president. Black residents walked or used car pools for 381 days. The courts (Browder v Gayle) ruled bus segregation unconstitutional, and the buses were integrated in December 1956.

Fact file

  • Lasted 381 days.
  • Browder v Gayle: ruled in June 1956 and upheld by the Supreme Court in November 1956.
  • Led to the founding of the SCLC in 1957.

Timeline

  1. 1 Dec 1955Parks arrested.
  2. 5 Dec 1955Boycott begins.
  3. Nov 1956Supreme Court upholds Browder.
  4. Dec 1956Buses integrated.

Significance and connotations

What mentioning this in an essay signals, and the arguments it supports.

  • Local activism. Parks was not just a tired seamstress; she was a trained activist. Grassroots organisation made the boycott work.
  • Protest plus courts. The boycott kept up pressure, but the legal ruling won it.

Event · September 1957

Little Rock

The President sends troops to enforce school integration.

What happened

Arkansas Governor Orval Faubus used the National Guard to stop nine Black students entering Little Rock Central High School. After a violent white mob gathered, President Eisenhower, who was privately unenthusiastic about Brown, sent the 101st Airborne Division to escort them in. Faubus closed the city's high schools in 1958–59 rather than integrate.

Fact file

  • The "Little Rock Nine".
  • About 1,000 paratroopers deployed; the Arkansas Guard was put under federal control.
  • The Civil Rights Act of 1957 was passed in the same month, the first since Reconstruction.

Timeline

  1. Sep 1957Faubus blocks entry; troops sent.
  2. 1958–59Schools closed.

Significance and connotations

What mentioning this in an essay signals, and the arguments it supports.

  • Federal reluctance, federal power. Even an unwilling president would enforce the law when states openly defied it.

Event · April – August 1963

Birmingham and the March on Washington

Televised brutality and a mass march push Kennedy to act.

What happened

In Birmingham, Alabama, King and the SCLC launched "Project C" to confront segregation in America's most segregated city. When adults were jailed, children marched in the "Children's Crusade" in May. Police chief Bull Connor turned dogs and fire hoses on them, in front of TV cameras. On 11 June Kennedy announced a civil rights bill. On 28 August around 250,000 people joined the March on Washington, where King gave his "I Have a Dream" speech. Weeks later a bomb at the 16th Street Baptist Church killed four girls.

Fact file

  • Letter from Birmingham Jail: April 1963.
  • Kennedy's televised civil rights address: 11 June 1963.
  • March organised by Randolph and Bayard Rustin.
  • Church bombing: 15 September 1963.

Timeline

  1. Apr 1963Birmingham campaign; King jailed.
  2. May 1963Children's Crusade.
  3. Jun 1963Kennedy's address.
  4. Aug 1963March on Washington.
  5. Sep 1963Church bombing.

Significance and connotations

What mentioning this in an essay signals, and the arguments it supports.

  • The media strategy. Birmingham is the clearest example of nonviolence provoking violence to win national sympathy and federal action.

Event · 1964–1965

The Civil Rights Act and the Voting Rights Act

Federal law ends legal segregation and protects the vote.

What happened

The Civil Rights Act (2 July 1964) banned segregation in public places and discrimination in employment, and created the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission. It passed after the longest filibuster in Senate history was broken. Voting remained the gap. After Freedom Summer (1964) and Selma's "Bloody Sunday" (7 March 1965), when marchers were beaten on the Edmund Pettus Bridge, the Voting Rights Act (6 August 1965) suspended literacy tests and sent federal examiners to register voters.

Fact file

  • The Senate filibuster lasted about two months before it was broken.
  • Twenty-fourth Amendment (1964) banned poll taxes in federal elections.
  • Black voter registration in Mississippi rose from about 7% in 1964 to about 60% in 1967.

Timeline

  1. Jul 1964Civil Rights Act.
  2. Summer 1964Freedom Summer; three activists murdered.
  3. Mar 1965Selma.
  4. Aug 1965Voting Rights Act.

Significance and connotations

What mentioning this in an essay signals, and the arguments it supports.

  • The legal end of Jim Crow. The high point of the movement, which makes it the obvious endpoint for questions on 1955–68.
  • Protest, President and Congress together. The best case for arguing several factors worked in combination.

Event · 1965–1970s

Black Power and the Black Panthers

A turn to pride, self-defence and community control.

What happened

The Watts riot in Los Angeles (August 1965) showed that Northern and Western cities faced problems the Southern movement had not solved: poverty, housing and police brutality. In June 1966 Stokely Carmichael of SNCC called for "Black Power". SNCC expelled its white members. In Oakland, Huey Newton and Bobby Seale founded the Black Panther Party (1966), which combined armed patrols of the police with community programmes such as free breakfasts. The FBI's COINTELPRO set out to destroy it.

Fact file

  • Watts: 34 dead. Detroit (1967): 43 dead.
  • The Kerner Commission (1968) warned the nation was "moving toward two societies, one black, one white".
  • Tommie Smith and John Carlos gave the Black Power salute at the 1968 Olympics.
  • Panther leader Fred Hampton was killed in a police raid in Chicago in 1969.

Timeline

  1. 1965Watts.
  2. 1966Black Power slogan; Panthers founded.
  3. 1967Detroit.
  4. 1968Kerner Report.

Significance and connotations

What mentioning this in an essay signals, and the arguments it supports.

  • Help or harm? It built pride and community institutions, but its rhetoric fed white backlash and Nixon's "law and order" politics.
