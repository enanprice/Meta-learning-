One essay plan for your teacher's second topic: **tsarism, the problems it faced, and its growing lack of support, 1914–17**. Dates are Old Style.

## Question: *How far was the First World War the main reason for the growing lack of support for tsarism in the years 1914–17?*

> **Line of argument:** the war was the **main reason**, because it caused the military defeats, economic collapse and food crisis that turned every group against the regime. But the war worked through **Nicholas II's own decisions**: taking command in 1915, leaving Alexandra and Rasputin in charge, and refusing to share power with the Duma. These turned military failure into political collapse. Long-term weaknesses (the 1912–14 strike wave, the hollowed-out Dumas) meant support was already fragile in 1914.

## Evidence bank

| Strand | Evidence |
|---|---|
| Military defeat | Tannenberg (August 1914): about 30,000 killed or wounded and about 90,000 captured. The Great Retreat (1915) lost Russian Poland. The Brusilov Offensive (1916) began well but ended in heavy losses |
| Scale | About 15 million men mobilised during the war. Several million killed, wounded or captured by early 1917 (estimates vary) |
| Supplies | 1915 shortages of shells and rifles; some troops went into battle without weapons |
| Economy | Prices roughly quadrupled between 1914 and early 1917. The government printed money. The railways could not move food and fuel to the cities |
| Food | Petrograd short of bread and fuel in the cold winter of 1916–17 |
| Vodka ban | Prohibition (1914) cut off a major source of state revenue from the vodka monopoly |
| The Tsar's choices | Took personal command at Mogilev (August 1915), so defeats became his fault. Left Alexandra in charge in Petrograd |
| Ministerial leapfrog | September 1915 to February 1917: four prime ministers and six interior ministers |
| Rasputin | Influence on appointments. Murdered by aristocrats in December 1916 |
| Elite opposition | Progressive Bloc (August 1915), a Duma majority demanding a "government of public confidence". Refused. Milyukov's speech asking "Is this stupidity or treason?" (1 November 1916) |
| Public organisations | Zemgor (led by Prince Lvov) and the War Industries Committees ran war supplies better than the government |
| February 1917 | Women's Day protests (23 February), a general strike, the Petrograd garrison mutiny (27 February), abdication (2 March) |

## Introduction

- **Define:** "lack of support" means the loss of loyalty among the groups tsarism relied on: the army, the elites and Duma politicians, the urban workers and the peasants.
- **Context:** in July 1914 the war began with patriotic rallies, and strikes stopped. Petersburg was even renamed Petrograd to sound less German. By February 1917 almost no one would defend the Tsar.
- **Judgement:** the war was the main reason, but its effects were made fatal by Nicholas's decisions and by weaknesses that already existed.

## Paragraph 1: The war's military and economic impact (main reason)

- **Point:** the war created the hardship and humiliation that destroyed support among soldiers, workers and peasants.
- **Evidence:**
  - Defeats at Tannenberg (1914) and in the Great Retreat (1915), with millions of casualties. Shortages of shells and rifles.
  - About 15 million men mobilised, taking labour from farms and factories.
  - Prices roughly quadrupled. The railways broke down. Petrograd ran short of bread and fuel in the winter of 1916–17.
- **Analysis:** ordinary Russians experienced the war as defeat, hunger and inflation. That turned the peasant army and the urban workers, the people who had to be controlled, against the regime.
- **Counter:** in 1916 the army was better supplied and the Brusilov Offensive had early success, so the war was not a story of pure collapse.
- **Mini-judgement:** the war was the underlying cause of lost support in 1914–17.

## Paragraph 2: Nicholas II's own decisions (made the war fatal)

- **Point:** the Tsar's choices turned military failure into a crisis of the monarchy.
- **Evidence:**
  - By taking command in August 1915 he became personally responsible for every defeat.
  - Alexandra, German-born and widely suspected, ran domestic government with Rasputin's influence. There were four prime ministers and six interior ministers in 17 months.
  - He refused the Progressive Bloc's request for a government the Duma could trust.
- **Analysis:** these decisions alienated the elites, the natural supporters of the monarchy. Rasputin's murder by aristocrats and Milyukov's "stupidity or treason?" speech show that the loyal upper classes had turned.
- **Counter:** these decisions only mattered because of the war. In peacetime they would not have been as damaging.
- **Mini-judgement:** the factor that turned a war crisis into the end of the dynasty.

## Paragraph 3: Long-term weaknesses (support was already fragile)

- **Point:** support had been weakening before 1914, so the war hit a regime that was already vulnerable.
- **Evidence:**
  - Strikers rose from about 725,000 (1912) to 1.3 million in January–July 1914, after the Lena Goldfields massacre (1912).
  - The Fundamental Laws and the 1907 electoral law had disappointed liberals.
  - Land hunger and peasant grievances remained after Stolypin's reforms.
- **Analysis:** this explains why support collapsed so quickly once the war went badly. The patriotism of 1914 covered deep problems for only a few months.
- **Counter:** the outbreak of war rallied people behind the Tsar, which suggests the pre-war problems could still be contained.
- **Mini-judgement:** important background, but not the main cause.

## Conclusion

- **Weigh it up:** the war (P1) created the crisis. Nicholas's decisions (P2) made it a crisis of the monarchy itself. Long-term problems (P3) meant support was fragile.
- **Final judgement:** the war was the main reason support collapsed in 1914–17, but it was fatal because of Nicholas's choices. The outcome showed in February 1917: workers struck, soldiers mutinied, and the generals and Duma leaders told the Tsar to go.

## Historians to mention

- **"The war as catalyst":** many historians argue the war sped up a collapse the regime's weaknesses made likely.
- **"The war as cause":** optimists argue that without the war, tsarism could have reformed and survived.
