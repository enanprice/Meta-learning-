## Question: *To what extent was the development of banking and insurance more important than the changing cloth trade to the success of the British economy in the years 1650–88? (20 marks)*

> **Line of argument:** only to a limited extent. In 1650–88 the **cloth trade was more important**: it was still most of London's exports and employed huge numbers through the domestic system. Banking and insurance were growing fast and helped trade work. But your textbook says insurance was "still in its infancy", and banking was badly set back by the Crown in 1672. Their real boom came after 1688. They mattered most as **support for trade**, much of which was cloth.

## Introduction

- **Define:** "banking and insurance" covers money scriveners, goldsmith-bankers, cheques and bills, and marine and fire insurance. "The changing cloth trade" covers the shift to the new draperies, immigrant skills and the domestic system. "Success" means growth in wealth, trade and employment.
- **Context:** by 1688 Britain's economy was "in a remarkably strong state" and London was the hub of a European market.
- **Judgement:** the cloth trade was more important in 1650–88. Banking and insurance were vital and growing, but still young, and their greatest impact came after 1688.

## Paragraph 1: Banking (growing importance, but held back)

- **Point:** banking grew rapidly after 1650 and provided the credit trade needed.
- **Evidence:**
  - The legal interest limit was cut to 6% from 1651, and by 1688 good loans cost 4–6%, so credit was cheaper.
  - Robert Abbott's scrivening firm handled £1,137,646 in 1652–55, lending royalist landowners' capital. His nephew Robert Clayton earned £3,515 a year in interest by 1672 and wrote the first English cheque in 1659.
  - Goldsmith-bankers rose from 32 (1670) to 44 (1677) and funded foreign ventures. Stephen Quinn argues their guild links made London banking stable.
- **Analysis:** cheaper credit let merchants fund voyages and trade, which links banking to cloth exports.
- **Counter:** in 1672 Charles II confiscated the goldsmiths' deposits, and public confidence was lost until William III repaid the loans in 1688. The Hale Commission's banking reforms were not acted on.
- **Mini-judgement:** important and growing, but damaged by the Crown. Its biggest impact came after 1688.

## Paragraph 2: Insurance (important, but in its infancy)

- **Point:** insurance reduced the risks of trade and made London a world centre, but it was still small.
- **Evidence:**
  - Marine insurance: by 1657 even Dutch ships were insured in London. Prices fell by up to 75%, and London became the leading insurance market. Adverts appeared from 1680, and Lloyd's opened in 1688.
  - Fire insurance grew after the Great Fire (1666): the Fire Office (1681) and the Friendly Society (1683).
  - Limits: many merchants insured only the minimum (William Freeman, 1680, Source 6). Some, such as the East India Company, didn't insure at all. Often only half a cargo's value was covered. There was no life or accident insurance.
- **Analysis:** the book's own verdict is that insurance "was still in its infancy", though "an essential ingredient" for future growth.
- **Mini-judgement:** an enabler of trade rather than a main source of wealth in 1650–88.

## Paragraph 3: The cloth trade (most important)

- **Point:** cloth was still the backbone of the economy, and its adaptation after 1650 kept it so.
- **Evidence:**
  - Cloth made up 74% of London's exports in 1660 and 72% in 1700. London alone exported £1.5 million of textiles in the 1660s.
  - The new draperies (bays and says, lighter mixtures of wool, linen, cotton and silk) replaced heavy broadcloth. Norwich and Colchester cloth was so trusted that buyers didn't check samples. Colchester's textile workforce reached 40% by 1699.
  - The domestic (putting-out) system employed families across the country. 240,000 people worked in skilled crafts by the end of the period.
  - The Navigation Act helped the new draperies dominate over the Dutch. Huguenot weavers after 1685 added new skills.
- **Analysis:** cloth created the wealth and the exports. Banking and insurance helped finance and protect them, so the two were linked, but cloth was the source of the value.
- **Counter:** cloth's share of exports was slowly falling (92% to 72%), a sign that the economy was diversifying.
- **Mini-judgement:** the most important factor in 1650–88.

## Conclusion

- **Weigh it up:** banking (P1) and insurance (P2) grew fast but were limited, by the 1672 confiscation and by being in their infancy. The cloth trade (P3) still produced most of the export wealth.
- **Final judgement:** banking and insurance were less important than the cloth trade in 1650–88, though they were becoming more important. They served the trade that cloth dominated. Their real impact on Britain's success came after 1688, with Lloyd's, the Bank of England (1694) and the financial growth of the 18th century.

## Historians (from your textbook)

- **D.C. Coleman:** textile exports multiplied 15-fold between 1485 and 1714, outpacing population and prices.
- **Stephen Quinn:** the guild system gave goldsmith-bankers familiarity and reputation, which stabilised London banking.
- **Revisionist historians (since the 1970s):** London's economy grew through many small developments at once, not one big breakthrough.
