## Question: *To what extent did the British economy radically change in the years 1625–88?*

> **Line of argument:** only to a limited extent overall, but unevenly. **Trade, finance and empire** changed radically: London became the centre of European commerce, credit became widely available, and the Navigation Acts and colonies reshaped overseas trade. **Agriculture and cloth** changed significantly but gradually, building on older methods. And much **did not change**: population growth slowed, the putting-out system continued, transport improved only piecemeal, heavy industry barely existed, and Ireland was left behind. The book's verdict: 1625–88 was the "seedbed" for growth, not the transformation itself.

## Introduction

- **Define:** "radically change" means a fundamental change in how the economy worked: where wealth came from, how trade and finance were organised, how people earned a living. Growth alone isn't enough.
- **Context:** by 1688 the economy was "in a remarkably strong state" despite decades of war and revolution. But the commercial and industrial revolutions of the 18th and 19th centuries were still to come.
- **Judgement:** commerce, finance and empire changed radically. Agriculture and cloth changed gradually. Industry, transport and much of the regional economy did not, so overall the change was significant but not radical.

## Paragraph 1: Trade, finance and empire (radical change)

- **Point:** the commercial side of the economy changed fundamentally.
- **Evidence:**
  - London became the centre of European trade and finance, and by mid-century set national prices for livestock, grain and cloth.
  - Credit became widely available "for the first time": cheaper interest (6% from 1651), scriveners such as Abbott (£1.1 million in 1652–55), goldsmith-bankers and the first cheque (1659).
  - The Navigation Acts enforced English supremacy at sea against the Dutch. Customs revenue rose three and a half times between 1643 and 1659.
  - Empire brought tobacco, sugar and the triangular trade. East India Company profits rose six-fold.
- **Analysis:** this was a genuine change in where wealth came from and how it was organised. Ralph Davis calls it a "commercial revolution".
- **Counter:** banking was set back by the 1672 confiscation, insurance was still in its infancy, and the biggest gains from sugar and slavery came after 1688.
- **Mini-judgement:** the most radical change of the period, though not complete by 1688.

## Paragraph 2: Agriculture and cloth (significant but gradual change)

- **Point:** the productive base changed significantly, but by evolution rather than revolution.
- **Evidence:**
  - Enclosure and rotation cut fallow land to 1.8 million of 9 million acres. Clover, turnips and water meadows spread, and horses doubled to 630,000.
  - Capital investment created large commercial farms, and regions specialised.
  - The cloth trade revived through the new draperies (Norwich, Colchester), Dutch and Huguenot skills, and the frame knitting machine.
- **Analysis:** these changes raised output and fed London. But many techniques were older (water meadows, Tudor beginnings), and the book calls the cloth industry's general trend "a continuation of what already existed".
- **Counter:** for those who lost common land, such as at Sherrington and Wootton Bassett, the change was radical. Hill and Stone see it as the move to capitalism.
- **Mini-judgement:** significant change, but gradual.

## Paragraph 3: Continuity and limits (against radical change)

- **Point:** large parts of the economy hardly changed, and progress was uneven.
- **Evidence:**
  - Population growth slowed after 1650, and economic progress slowed with it.
  - The putting-out system continued, and farming stayed conservative in some regions.
  - Transport improved only piecemeal: turnpikes late in the period, unreliable rivers.
  - Heavy industry was "virtually non-existent", and coal and tin mining were slow and inefficient.
  - Ireland saw little investment or improvement. Scotland and Ireland had no national markets.
- **Analysis:** the industrial base that would define the later revolutions didn't exist yet. Change was concentrated in London and the commercial elite, and not felt by everyone.
- **Mini-judgement:** strong evidence against radical change across the economy as a whole.

## Conclusion

- **Weigh it up:** commerce and finance (P1) changed radically. Farming and cloth (P2) changed gradually. Industry, transport and the regions (P3) changed little.
- **Final judgement:** the economy changed significantly but not radically between 1625 and 1688. The most radical changes were commercial and financial and centred on London. Most of the economy evolved rather than transformed. The book's word "seedbed" fits: these years prepared for the transformations of the 18th century.

- **Christopher Hill and Lawrence Stone** (Marxist): the 17th century saw the transition from feudalism to an early capitalist system.
- **Revisionists since the 1970s:** London grew through many small developments together, not one big breakthrough.
