## Question: *"The republic failed in the years 1649–1660 because it depended on the army." Assess the validity of this view.*

> **Line of argument:** the view is largely valid. Dependence on the army was the **fundamental** weakness: it made every regime look illegitimate to the gentry who ran the country, and when the army split in 1659 nothing else held the state together. Lack of support for religious radicalism and the cost of the regime were serious problems, but both came back to the army. Cromwell's death was the **trigger**, because he was the only man who could control the army and still talk to Parliament.

## Introduction

- **Define:** "the republic" covers every regime from 1649 to 1660: the Rump, the Nominated Assembly, the Protectorate and the chaos of 1659. "Failed" means it could not find a settlement the political nation would accept, so the monarchy came back.
- **Context:** the regicide was carried out by an army-backed minority after Pride's Purge, so the republic began without broad consent.
- **Judgement:** dependence on the army was the fundamental reason. It made the regime unacceptable to the gentry and Parliament, and the army's divisions in 1659 destroyed it. Religious division and finance mattered, but each was shaped by the army's role. Cromwell's death in 1658 was the short-term trigger.

## Paragraph 1: Dependence on the army (the fundamental cause)

- **Point:** every regime existed because the army allowed it, and was removed when the army chose. That is the root of the failure.
- **Evidence:**
  - Pride's Purge (1648) created the Rump; Cromwell and his soldiers ended it in April 1653.
  - The Instrument of Government (1653) was written by an army officer, Lambert, and guaranteed an army of 30,000.
  - The Major-Generals (1655–57) put soldiers in charge of the counties, enforcing order and godly behaviour, and paid for them with the decimation tax on royalists.
  - In 1659 the army forced Richard Cromwell out, recalled the Rump, expelled it again in October, and then split. Monck's army decided the outcome.
- **Analysis:** the gentry who ran local government saw military rule as the opposite of the "ancient constitution". The Major-Generals made the army's power visible in every county and turned moderate opinion toward the monarchy. Yet the regime could not do without the army, because it had no other base of support.
- **Counter:** the army was not always the problem. Under Cromwell it was disciplined and loyal, and the Protectorate gave England stability from 1653 to 1658.
- **Mini-judgement:** the army explains why no regime could become legitimate, and it was the army's divisions in 1659 that ended the republic.

## Paragraph 2: Lack of support and religious division (a serious, linked factor)

- **Point:** the republic never won over the people who mattered: royalists, Presbyterians and most gentry. Its association with religious radicalism frightened them further.
- **Evidence:**
  - The Nominated Assembly (1653) tried to abolish tithes and the Court of Chancery. Gentry saw that as an attack on property.
  - Sects multiplied: Quakers, Fifth Monarchists and Baptists. James Nayler's re-enactment of Christ's entry into Jerusalem at Bristol (1656) caused a panic in Parliament.
  - Parliaments elected under the Protectorate attacked its constitution (1654) and had to be purged (1656).
  - The Humble Petition and Advice (1657) tried to restore a monarchy in all but name, showing what the political nation wanted.
- **Analysis:** Cromwell wanted "liberty of conscience" for godly Protestants. Most MPs wanted order and a national church. The army was the main protector of the sects, so fear of radicalism was also fear of the army.
- **Counter:** Cromwell's broad church kept most of the parish system working, and the gentry were not ready to rebel until 1659.
- **Mini-judgement:** a major cause, but closely tied to the army, which protected radicals and stopped a conservative settlement.

## Paragraph 3: Cromwell's death and the crisis of 1659 (the trigger)

- **Point:** the republic held together while Cromwell lived. His death removed the only person both the army and Parliament would follow.
- **Evidence:**
  - Cromwell died on 3 September 1658. Richard had no military record and no hold on the officers.
  - By April 1659 the Wallingford House group of officers had forced Richard to dissolve Parliament.
  - Booth's royalist rising (August 1659) was crushed, which shows the republic could still beat royalists. It could not stop its own army splitting.
  - Monck's march from Scotland on 1 January 1660 ended the republic. He restored the secluded members, who called new elections, and the new Convention invited Charles back.
- **Analysis:** Cromwell's personal authority covered the regime's structural weakness. Once it was gone, the generals competed with each other and with the Rump, and the country turned to the king as the only source of order.
- **Counter:** Cromwell himself never found a settlement. He dissolved every Parliament he called, so the failure was not only Richard's.
- **Mini-judgement:** the death was the trigger, but it mattered only because everything depended on the army, which only Cromwell could manage.

## Conclusion

- **Weigh it up:** religion and lack of support explain why the republic was unpopular. Cromwell's death explains when it collapsed. The army explains both: it blocked a conservative settlement, and its split in 1659 brought the regime down.
- **Final judgement:** the view is valid. The republic failed because it rested on military force rather than consent. In the end it was a soldier, Monck, who brought back the king, because the army was the only power left in the state.

## Historians

- **Blair Worden:** the Rump failed to reform because it was a conservative body, not a revolutionary one.
- **Austin Woolrych:** the Protectorate's search for settlement was serious, and Cromwell was a genuine, if frustrated, constitutionalist.
- **Ronald Hutton:** stresses the chaos of 1659. The Restoration resulted from the army's collapse rather than a royalist revival.
- **Barry Coward:** the Protectorate was more successful and more conservative than its reputation suggests.
