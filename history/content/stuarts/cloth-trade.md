One essay plan on the cloth trade. The key idea is the difference between the **old draperies** and the **new draperies**:

- **Old draperies:** heavy woollen broadcloth, mostly exported undyed and unfinished to the Netherlands and Germany.
- **New draperies:** lighter, cheaper worsteds and mixed cloths (bays, says and serges), sold to southern Europe and the Mediterranean.

## Question: *How far did the English cloth trade decline in the years 1603–1702?*

> **Line of argument:** the **old broadcloth trade** declined sharply, especially after the Cockayne Project and the depression of the 1620s. But the cloth trade **as a whole adapted rather than declined**: the new draperies, new markets and new regions took over. What really changed was cloth's **share** of the economy. By 1700 England's overseas trade was far more diverse, so cloth mattered less, even though it was still the largest single export.

## Evidence bank

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| c.1600 | Woollen cloth makes up around three-quarters or more of English exports, mostly unfinished broadcloth sent via London by the Merchant Adventurers |
| 1614–17 | Cockayne Project: export of unfinished cloth banned; the Dutch refuse English finished cloth; exports collapse; the scheme is abandoned |
| 1620–24 | Trade depression: currency debasement in Germany and the Baltic, and the Thirty Years' War, wreck the old markets |
| 1620s–40s | New draperies grow, centred on Norwich and Colchester and built on the skills of Dutch and Walloon immigrants ("Strangers") |
| 1640s | Civil war disrupts trade, especially through London |
| 1651, 1660 | Navigation Acts: English goods to be carried in English ships |
| 1652–74 | Three Anglo-Dutch wars, fought largely over trade |
| 1660–62 | Export of raw wool banned to protect English clothmakers |
| 1666, 1678 | Burying in Woollen Acts: the dead had to be buried in woollen shrouds to boost demand |
| 1685 onwards | Huguenot refugees bring silk weaving to Spitalfields and other skills |
| 1700 | Calico Act bans imports of printed Indian cotton cloth to protect wool and silk |

- **Around 1700:** woollens were still the largest export, but re-exports of colonial and Asian goods (tobacco, sugar, calicoes) made up roughly a third of all exports.
- **Regional shift:** the old broadcloth areas of Suffolk and Essex declined. Devon serges (around Exeter) and the West Riding of Yorkshire grew.

## Introduction

- **Define:** "decline" can mean an absolute fall in output and exports, or a relative fall in importance compared with the rest of the economy. The answer differs for each.
- **Context:** in 1603 cloth was by far England's main export and employed huge numbers of spinners and weavers, many of them women working at home.
- **Judgement:** the old trade in unfinished broadcloth declined. The cloth industry as a whole changed and survived, and cloth fell in relative importance as English trade diversified.

## Paragraph 1: The decline of the old draperies (for decline)

- **Point:** the traditional trade that dominated in 1603 suffered a serious and lasting decline.
- **Evidence:**
  - The Cockayne Project (1614–17), an attempt to make England finish its own cloth, led the Dutch to boycott English finished cloth. London's cloth exports fell sharply and the scheme collapsed.
  - The depression of 1620–24 hit the German and Baltic markets. The Privy Council set up a commission on the decay of trade in 1622.
  - The old broadcloth districts of Suffolk and Essex, such as Lavenham, never recovered their Tudor wealth.
  - Civil war in the 1640s, and Dutch competition and wars after 1650, disrupted trade further.
- **Analysis:** the old trade relied on a narrow product, unfinished heavy cloth, in a narrow set of northern European markets. That made it vulnerable to political mistakes and foreign competition.
- **Counter:** the decline was in one part of the trade. It was not the whole story.
- **Mini-judgement:** a genuine and permanent decline, for the old draperies.

## Paragraph 2: Adaptation: new draperies, new markets (against decline)

- **Point:** the industry adapted by making different cloth for different markets.
- **Evidence:**
  - The new draperies grew in Norwich, Colchester and later Devon, using Dutch and Walloon skills.
  - They sold to Spain, Italy and the eastern Mediterranean, through the Levant Company, where lighter cloth suited the climate.
  - The West Riding of Yorkshire expanded its cheaper woollens.
  - Government protected the industry: bans on raw wool exports (1660s), the Burying in Woollen Acts (1666, 1678) and the Calico Act (1700).
- **Analysis:** these changes show an industry restructuring rather than collapsing. Government policy shows cloth was still seen as vital to the nation's wealth.
- **Counter:** protection itself suggests the industry was under pressure, especially from Indian calicoes in the 1690s.
- **Mini-judgement:** the strongest argument against decline. The industry survived by changing.

## Paragraph 3: Diversification: cloth's relative decline (the best explanation)

- **Point:** the most important change was that cloth stopped dominating the economy.
- **Evidence:**
  - The Navigation Acts (1651, 1660) built a protected colonial trade.
  - Re-exports of tobacco, sugar and Asian textiles grew rapidly after 1660. By 1700 they made up roughly a third of all exports.
  - The East India Company's imports of calicoes and silks created new fashions and new competition.
  - Huguenot refugees after 1685 strengthened silk and other industries.
- **Analysis:** this "commercial revolution" (Ralph Davis's phrase) made English trade broader and less dependent on one product. Cloth grew less than the rest of trade, so its share fell even if its output did not.
- **Counter:** cloth was still the largest single export in 1700 and the biggest manufacturing employer.
- **Mini-judgement:** a relative decline, caused by the growth of other trades rather than the failure of cloth.

## Conclusion

- **Weigh it up:** the old draperies (P1) did decline, but the new draperies (P2) show adaptation. The biggest change (P3) was cloth's falling share of a growing, diversifying economy.
- **Final judgement:** the cloth trade declined only to a limited extent. The old trade collapsed and was replaced. Cloth's dominance ended, not because cloth failed but because English trade became more varied and global.

## Historians

- **Barry Supple** (*Commercial Crisis and Change in England, 1600–1642*): the early-century crises were serious and exposed the weakness of the old trade.
- **Ralph Davis:** a "commercial revolution" after 1660 transformed English trade through re-exports and colonial goods.
- **D.C. Coleman:** the new draperies show the industry's ability to adapt.
