## Question: *How significant were improved agricultural techniques for the success of the British economy, 1650–88? (20 marks)*

> **Line of argument:** very significant. Better farming was the **foundation** of the economy's success. It fed a fast-growing London, released labour for industry, and made specialised farming and national markets possible. But it did not work alone. Its effects depended on **capital investment** and **transport**, its gains were **uneven** (labourers and small farmers often lost out), and trade and empire also drove growth. So: the most significant domestic factor, not the only cause.

## Introduction

- **Define:** "improved agricultural techniques" means enclosure, better crop rotation, new crops, water meadows, drainage and more horses. "Success" means growth in output, trade, towns and living standards.
- **Context:** population growth earlier in the century had created a need to grow more food to avoid famine. After 1650 population growth slowed and prices fell, which made it cheaper for big landowners to invest.
- **Judgement:** very significant, as the base everything else stood on. But techniques mattered because they were combined with capital investment and better transport, and their benefits were unevenly shared.

## Paragraph 1: Higher output fed a growing economy (significant)

- **Point:** new techniques produced much more food, which allowed towns, above all London, to grow.
- **Evidence:**
  - Enclosure let farmers rotate more fields and leave less land fallow. Fallow fell from 3 million acres (1420) to 1.8 million (1700) while arable grew to 9 million acres.
  - Clover and turnips restored the soil. Water meadows gave animals early grass and hay for winter.
  - Horses doubled to about 630,000, and they were quicker than oxen.
  - Grain shipped to London more than doubled between 1605 and 1661.
- **Analysis:** more food meant famine was less of a threat and London could keep growing, to around 575,000 by 1700. The book says improved agriculture "meant that more food was produced, which in turn meant that London and other large towns were able to expand."
- **Counter:** many of these techniques started earlier (water meadows and drainage) or spread slowly (potatoes were not widespread). Blith's book came out in 1649, so the real spread was after 1650.
- **Mini-judgement:** this is the strongest case for significance. Without more food, urban and commercial growth was impossible.

## Paragraph 2: Specialisation, investment and markets (significant, with conditions)

- **Point:** techniques mattered most when combined with capital investment and new markets. That turned farming into a commercial business.
- **Evidence:**
  - Gentry and aristocrats bought and enclosed land and invested in new crops. In the south Midlands, estates over 100 acres rose from 32.2% to 53.6% of farmland. Some East Anglian farms were over 500 acres.
  - Regions specialised (Figure 4.1): wheat and hops in the South East, sheep in Yorkshire, cheese in Cheshire, dairy in Wiltshire and Somerset.
  - Transport improved: the Turnpike Act (1663), stage coaches (1657), Ogilby's *Britannia* (1675), and rivers such as the Severn, Ouse and Thames.
  - London set national prices for livestock, grain and cloth by mid-century.
- **Analysis:** the techniques alone weren't enough. Investment and transport let farmers sell to distant markets where demand was high. That links agriculture to the growth of trade and to industry, such as wool and flax for textiles.
- **Counter:** national markets did not dominate until the late 18th century, many farmers still sold locally, and Scotland and Ireland lacked national markets.
- **Mini-judgement:** significant, but its impact depended on investment and infrastructure.

## Paragraph 3: Limits and other factors (less significant than it looks)

- **Point:** agricultural change had real costs, and other factors drove the economy too.
- **Evidence:**
  - Small tenants lost out. Wage labourers grew to 364,000 families by 1688, and inflation cut real wages for much of the century.
  - Sherrington freeholders were driven out after 1660. The Wootton Bassett commoners (Source 1) were "put out of all common".
  - London's demand drained the South East: basic food prices in 1650 were six times their 1500 level, and London brewers destroyed brewing in small towns by outbidding them for barley.
  - Other factors: the cloth trade (still 74% of London's exports in 1660), the Navigation Acts, empire, and banking.
- **Analysis:** agriculture helped the economy grow but not everyone gained. Christopher Hill argues that enclosure increased corn production but deprived the poor of fuel and pasture, which forced them into wage labour. That fed industry, which is itself a kind of significance.
- **Counter:** even the losses fed growth, by creating a labour force for cloth and towns.
- **Mini-judgement:** agriculture was one of several engines. Trade and the cloth industry were at least as important for wealth.

## Conclusion

- **Weigh it up:** P1 shows agriculture was the foundation. P2 shows it needed investment and transport to pay off. P3 shows it was not the only driver, and its gains were unevenly spread.
- **Final judgement:** improved techniques were highly significant for the success of the economy in 1650–88. They were the necessary foundation, feeding London and supplying labour and raw materials. But their impact came through capital investment and markets, and trade and cloth did more to create wealth.

## Historians (from your textbook)

- **Christopher Hill:** enclosure increased production but made the poor dependent on wages. It "released labour for industry".
- **Christopher Hill and Lawrence Stone** (Marxist view): enclosure and capital investment mark the move from feudalism to an early capitalist system, benefiting the middle and upper gentry.
