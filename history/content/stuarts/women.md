One essay plan on the power of women across the Stuart century. These questions usually reward a **change over time** argument: what changed in the crisis of the 1640s–50s, and what was left by 1702?

## Question: *To what extent did women gain political influence in the years 1603–1702?*

> **Line of argument:** only to a limited extent. Women gained **visibility and informal influence**: at court, in the crisis of the civil wars, in the radical sects and, by the end of the century, in print and on the stage. But they gained no **formal** political power. The law of coverture, the vote and office stayed closed to them. The changes of the 1640s and 1650s were largely reversed after 1660. Even the queens of 1689 and 1702 ruled through a male political system.

- **Coverture:** a married woman's legal identity was absorbed into her husband's. She could not own property in her own right or make contracts.
- Sir Robert Filmer's *Patriarcha* (published 1680) defended royal power by comparing it to a father's rule over his family.

## Introduction

- **Define:** "political influence" means the ability to shape decisions about government. It can be *formal* (office, voting, law) or *informal* (persuasion, petitioning, patronage, print).
- **Context:** Stuart society was patriarchal. The household was seen as a little kingdom with the husband as king, and women were excluded from the vote, Parliament and the professions.
- **Judgement:** women's informal influence grew, especially in the crisis of 1640–60 and in the cultural world after 1660. Formal power did not change, and most of the gains of the revolutionary years were lost.

## Paragraph 1: Royal and court women (real influence, but derived from men)

- **Point:** the most powerful women of the century were royal women and courtiers, but their influence came through husbands, lovers and kings.
- **Evidence:**
  - Henrietta Maria shaped Charles I's court and his tolerance of Catholics, and was blamed by Parliament for his policies. In 1642–43 she raised arms abroad and was impeached by the Commons.
  - Charles II's mistresses, especially Barbara Villiers and Louise de Kérouaille, controlled patronage. Kérouaille was widely seen as the agent of Louis XIV.
  - Mary II was joint monarch from 1689 but chose to leave executive power to William, ruling only while he was away at war.
- **Analysis:** these women did influence policy, but their power depended on their relationships with men and was resented as illegitimate. Henrietta Maria's influence fed anti-Catholic panic; Kérouaille's fed fears of French influence.
- **Counter:** Queen Anne (from 1702) held real power in her own right and took an active part in politics.
- **Mini-judgement:** real influence for a very small elite, but always through and dependent on men.

## Paragraph 2: Crisis and revolution, 1640–60 (the high point)

- **Point:** the breakdown of authority in the civil wars gave ordinary and gentry women unprecedented roles.
- **Evidence:**
  - Gentry women defended their homes in war: Brilliana Harley at Brampton Bryan (1643), Mary Bankes at Corfe, the Countess of Derby at Lathom (1644).
  - Women petitioned Parliament in large numbers: the peace protests of 1642–43, and the Leveller women's petitions of 1649, organised by figures such as Elizabeth Lilburne and Katherine Chidley.
  - Radical sects let women preach and prophesy. Quakers gave women a formal role in their meetings.
- **Analysis:** these were real political actions, and contemporaries were alarmed by them. But they happened because men were absent or because order had collapsed, and they were often defended as wifely duty rather than as women's rights. The Commons dismissed the Leveller women and told them to go home.
- **Counter:** the experience was not lost. Quaker women kept their role, and Margaret Fell's *Women's Speaking Justified* (1666) defended it in print.
- **Mini-judgement:** the high point of women's political activity, but temporary and dependent on crisis.

## Paragraph 3: Structural limits and the Restoration (continuity)

- **Point:** the structures that excluded women did not change, and the Restoration reinforced patriarchy.
- **Evidence:**
  - Coverture, exclusion from the vote and from office, and limited education continued throughout.
  - The Restoration repressed the sects that gave women a voice (Clarendon Code, 1661–65).
  - The witch-hunts of the 1640s show how vulnerable women became when order broke down.
  - New cultural openings: actresses after 1660, Aphra Behn, Margaret Cavendish, and Mary Astell's call for women's education in 1694.
- **Analysis:** the cultural changes were the most lasting gains, because print and the stage gave women a public voice. But Astell's demand for education shows how much was still missing in 1694. None of this was formal political power.
- **Counter:** widows and some wealthy women did control property and business, and could exercise local influence through patronage.
- **Mini-judgement:** formal power did not change, and the gains of the 1640s–50s were largely lost.

## Conclusion

- **Weigh it up:** the revolutionary years (P2) show the most women's political action, but it was temporary. Court influence (P1) was real but derivative. The structures (P3) didn't change.
- **Final judgement:** women gained political influence only to a limited extent. The century saw more women acting politically than ever before, but their influence stayed informal and dependent on men. The most lasting change was the growth of women's voices in print, which looked ahead to the eighteenth century.

## Historians

- **Patricia Crawford and Sara Mendelson** (*Women in Early Modern England*): women had more agency than the formal rules suggest, but within firm limits.
- **Anthony Fletcher** (*Gender, Sex and Subordination*): patriarchy adapted and survived the seventeenth century.
- **Keith Thomas:** the sects offered women a temporary escape from male authority.
