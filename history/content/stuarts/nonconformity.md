One essay plan on religious nonconformity from the Restoration to the death of Charles II. "Nonconformists" or "Dissenters" were Protestants who would not accept the restored Church of England: Presbyterians, Independents (Congregationalists), Baptists and Quakers. Catholics were also outside the Church, but are usually treated separately as "recusants". See the [Restoration](#stuarts-f-restoration) and [Charles II](#stuarts-f-charles-ii) fact files for context.

## Question: *How successful were attempts to suppress religious nonconformity in the years 1660–1685?*

> **Line of argument:** only partly successful. The laws known as the Clarendon Code drove Dissenters out of the Church, the universities and town government, and nonconformity shrank to a small minority. But they **failed to destroy** it. Enforcement was uneven, Charles II himself kept undermining persecution, and the Dissenters survived and organised. By 1685 nonconformity was a permanent part of English life, which the Toleration Act of 1689 would accept.

## Introduction

- **Define:** "suppress" could mean wiping nonconformity out, or excluding Dissenters from power and forcing them into conformity. Success looks different under each definition.
- **Context:** the Restoration brought back the bishops and the Prayer Book. To the Anglican gentry, Dissenters were the people who had caused the civil war and killed the king. Venner's rising (1661) seemed to prove it.
- **Judgement:** suppression succeeded in excluding Dissenters from public life and keeping them a small minority. It failed to destroy them, because Crown and Parliament disagreed, enforcement was patchy, and Dissenters proved resilient.

## Paragraph 1: The legal attack (success in exclusion)

- **Point:** the Clarendon Code shut Dissenters out of every public institution.
- **Evidence:**
  - Corporation Act (1661): purged town governments.
  - Act of Uniformity (1662): about 2,000 ministers ejected, around a fifth of the clergy.
  - Conventicle Acts (1664, 1670) and Five Mile Act (1665): attacked worship and preaching outside the Church.
  - Oxford and Cambridge were closed to Dissenters.
- **Analysis:** the laws worked in their main aim. Dissenters lost office, livings and education. The Compton Census suggests they were a small minority by 1676, though it probably undercounted people who went to both church and meeting.
- **Counter:** the code was named after Clarendon, who in fact favoured some compromise with Presbyterians. The drive came from the Cavalier Parliament.
- **Mini-judgement:** successful in excluding Dissenters, the realistic aim of most Anglicans.

## Paragraph 2: The limits: Crown policy and local enforcement (failure to destroy)

- **Point:** persecution was inconsistent, because the king did not support it and local enforcement varied.
- **Evidence:**
  - Charles II promised toleration at Breda and issued Declarations of Indulgence in 1662 and 1672. About 1,600 licences were issued in 1672, and they weren't revoked until 1675.
  - Enforcement depended on local JPs and constables, many of whom were sympathetic or unwilling. Towns like London and Bristol had strong Dissenting communities.
  - Persecution came in waves: 1662–65, 1670, and 1681–85. There were long periods of relative calm.
- **Analysis:** Charles's motives were partly to help Catholics, but his Indulgences gave Dissenters breathing space and showed persecution lacked royal backing. Divided authority meant suppression was never sustained.
- **Counter:** when Crown and Parliament agreed, as in the Tory reaction of 1681–85, persecution could be severe.
- **Mini-judgement:** the main reason suppression failed to destroy nonconformity.

## Paragraph 3: Dissenters' resilience and the politics of 1678–85 (survival)

- **Point:** Dissenters survived because they were committed and organised, and they became politically important.
- **Evidence:**
  - Bunyan wrote in prison and published *Pilgrim's Progress* (1678).
  - The Quakers created a national organisation, the Meeting for Sufferings (1675), to record persecution and lobby for relief.
  - Dissenters backed the Whigs in the Exclusion Crisis (1679–81). This led to the fierce Tory reaction of 1681–85, with mass imprisonments of Quakers and others.
- **Analysis:** persecution strengthened Dissenters' identity, and linking dissent to Whig politics made it a political force. Yet even the harshest wave (1681–85) did not break them.
- **Counter:** by 1685 Dissenters were politically defeated, the Whigs were crushed, and many Dissenters had conformed.
- **Mini-judgement:** nonconformity was weakened but permanent.

## Conclusion

- **Weigh it up:** the laws (P1) succeeded in excluding Dissenters. Inconsistent enforcement and Charles's own policy (P2) explain why they were never destroyed. Their resilience (P3) shows the survival was earned.
- **Final judgement:** suppression was only partly successful. It turned nonconformity into a small, excluded minority but could not eliminate it. The Toleration Act of 1689 was in effect an admission that persecution had failed.

## Historians

- **John Spurr:** the Restoration Church was strong, and persecution was driven by Anglican fear of renewed revolution.
- **Michael Watts** (*The Dissenters*): Dissent survived through deep conviction and organisation.
- **Ronald Hutton:** Charles II's religious policy was pragmatic and inconsistent rather than driven by principle.
